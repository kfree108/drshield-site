The rate, read from the page most reviews skip

Refunds Manager's homepage does not carry a pricing table, which is why several rankings for this keyword state that no rate is published. Its own FAQ page tells a different story: app.refundsmanager.com/FAQ.html, read 21 August 2026, states plainly: "We charge 25% of whatever reimbursements we recover. We only bill for a reimbursement once we've confirmed it's been deposited in your account, or the inventory has been replaced." The rate is published. It simply is not on the marketing homepage, which is a real lesson in where to look before concluding a vendor discloses nothing.

For example, if a vendor recovers $10,000 for you, a 25% rate means $2,500 goes to the vendor and $7,500 stays with you. That is a worked illustration, not a quote from anyone.

The billing clause that deserves real credit

The same FAQ page adds a second sentence that matters more than the headline rate: "We only take a percentage of cases for which our team provided the return (so you are not billed for auto-reimbursements Amazon makes)." Amazon now reimburses some lost-inventory cases on its own without a claim being filed, so this distinction is not a technicality. It is the difference between paying a vendor for work it did and paying a percentage on money Amazon was always going to send you. Few vendors in this category state the exclusion this explicitly, and it is worth asking any reimbursement vendor, including us, to put the same wording in writing.

The review record, and why it is the strongest one in this category

Trustpilot lists a TrustScore of 4.9 from 389 reviews for Refunds Manager, read 21 August 2026. That matches the figure the company advertises on its own homepage, which is a useful check: the third-party score and the self-reported one agree. Among the reimbursement specialists we researched, it is the largest public review record we found, and a high score sustained across that many reviews is a rare combination in this niche.

Refunds Manager's homepage also advertises figures like "recover up to 95%," "$800M recovered" and a "99% success rate." Those are the company's own claims on its homepage, not independently verified figures, so read them as marketing. The Trustpilot record is the part of this review that comes from a third party.

Where reimbursement money actually comes from

Knowing the categories helps you judge any vendor's claim. Across the seller accounts we manage, between 10 February and 21 August 2026, the money that came back from Amazon broke down by value like this (our own figures, from the Full Circle master database, stated net of reversals):

  • Customer returns never returned: $508,269 across 19,200 items
  • Lost inbound: $215,676 across 1,271 items
  • Customer service issues: $102,858 across 4,202 items
  • Damaged in warehouse: $83,426 across 5,594 items
  • Lost in warehouse: $62,818 across 5,233 items

In total that is $803,457 net of $170,047 in reversals ($973,504 gross), over 42,096 line items. Any vendor's headline recovery number, Refunds Manager's included, is more meaningful when it is stated net rather than gross, and it is a fair question to ask any recovery specialist how their published totals are calculated.

The pivot toward 1P vendor recovery, and what it means for a 3P seller

Refunds Manager is increasingly leading with 1P vendor recovery (shortages, co-op fees and chargebacks for brands selling wholesale to Amazon Vendor Central) while continuing to run its original FBA seller service. If your business sells only 3P through Seller Central, the core reimbursement service is unaffected by that positioning shift. If you straddle both 1P and 3P, one vendor covering both sides under a strong review record is a genuine convenience worth weighing.

What the review record does not tell you

A 4.9 average across 389 reviews is strong evidence that Refunds Manager delivers its stated job reliably. It says nothing about work outside that job. Like most reimbursement specialists, Refunds Manager is scoped to money Amazon owes for lost, damaged or mishandled inventory, not to suppressed listings, variation and Brand Registry problems, or account-health cases. A seller judging vendor quality purely by review score risks assuming a strong recovery specialist also covers ground it was never built to cover.

Six questions to ask any reimbursement vendor

Use this checklist on Refunds Manager, on us, or on anyone else:

  • What percentage do you take, and is it on the page or only on a call?
  • Do you bill on reimbursements Amazon issues automatically, or only on cases your team filed?
  • Is the fee charged when a claim is filed, or only after the money is deposited?
  • Are your published recovery totals gross or net of reversals?
  • Which claim types do you cover (lost inbound, warehouse damage, customer returns never returned, fee overcharges)?
  • What happens if I need help with a listing, fee or Brand Registry problem that is not a reimbursement?

Refunds Manager answers the first three clearly on its FAQ page, which is more than many vendors manage. Checking support and terms pages, not just the homepage, is a good habit for every vendor you evaluate.

Which one you should actually pick

Refunds Manager suits a seller who wants reimbursement recovery with a strong public review record and a billing policy that protects against paying for Amazon's own auto-reimbursements. It is a well-evidenced choice for that specific job, and one we would not talk a reader out of. For an account that also needs fee disputes, listing casework or Brand Registry protection, that sits outside Refunds Manager's scope, and it is where Shield's broader casework is built to work.

What to do with this

Judge this on the job you need done, not the feature list. Ask how they check a claim before filing it so Amazon does not reverse it later, and what they do when a case is closed without an answer. A service that only files claims is a different purchase from one that argues them to the end.

Common questions

How much does Refunds Manager charge?

25% of whatever reimbursements it recovers, per the company's own FAQ page, billed only once the reimbursement is confirmed deposited in your account or the inventory is replaced. The rate does not appear on the marketing homepage, but it is published on the FAQ page.

Does Refunds Manager charge for reimbursements Amazon issues automatically?

No. Its FAQ page states that it only takes a percentage of cases its own team filed, so you are not billed for Amazon's automatic reimbursements.

Is Refunds Manager's 4.9-star Trustpilot rating trustworthy?

It is the largest public review record we found in this category: 389 reviews at a 4.9 average, read 21 August 2026, matching the figure the company advertises on its own site. It is a strong signal, though it reflects reimbursement work specifically, not any broader casework.

Does Refunds Manager handle 1P Vendor Central recovery too?

Yes. It has increasingly pivoted toward 1P vendor recovery (shortages, co-op fees, chargebacks) alongside its original 3P FBA seller service, which suits a brand that sells both wholesale and through Seller Central.

Does Refunds Manager cover fee disputes or Brand Registry cases?

No. Like most of the reimbursement category, it is scoped to money Amazon already owes for lost, damaged or mishandled inventory. Fee disputes, suppressed listings, variation protection and Brand Registry casework sit outside that scope.

Is Refunds Manager's "$800M recovered" figure independently verified?

No. It is a self-reported figure on the company's own homepage, not an independently audited total. Treat it, along with the "up to 95%" and "99% success rate" claims on the same page, as marketing. The Trustpilot record is the third-party part of this review.
