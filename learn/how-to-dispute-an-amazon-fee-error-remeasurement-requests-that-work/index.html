What a remeasurement dispute actually does

Filing a fee dispute doesn't ask Amazon to accept your number — it asks Amazon to physically or procedurally re-verify the item's dimensions and weight and re-apply the current rate card to whatever it finds. That's an important distinction, because it means the outcome isn't guaranteed to favor you even when you're confident the current fee is wrong. If your evidence is accurate and the current fee really is based on a stale or incorrect measurement, a remeasurement should correct it. If your evidence is wrong, or the packaging genuinely did grow since the last measurement, a remeasurement can just as easily confirm — or raise — the existing fee.

This is different from a reimbursement claim in a way worth being explicit about. A reimbursement claim asks Amazon to pay for a specific, past loss — the downside of a weak claim is a denial, and the fee schedule going forward is unaffected either way. A fee dispute asks Amazon to re-set an ongoing, per-unit rate — the downside of a weak or premature dispute is a rate that goes up and then applies to every future sale until it's disputed again. That asymmetry is the reason a fee dispute deserves more upfront verification than a reimbursement claim does, not less.

The two questions to answer before filing anything

Before opening a case, answer two questions honestly. First: does your own measurement of the current shipped package actually support a different tier or weight than what's billed — not what the product weighed at launch, not what the spec sheet says, but the real box shipping today? Second: has the packaging changed since Amazon last measured it, in a direction that would make the current fee more accurate, not less? Skipping either question is how a seller ends up filing a dispute that gets denied, or worse, one that gets the fee raised instead of lowered.

The evidence that actually moves a case

  • Photos of the sealed, shipped package next to a tape measure on all three dimensions, not a stock photo or a spec sheet render.
  • A scale reading of the actual shipped weight, including any inserts, void fill, or poly bag — the full package as it ships, not the bare product.
  • The specific fee currently being charged and the size tier it implies, pulled from your own fee report rather than described from memory.
  • The size tier and fee you believe should apply, calculated from your own measurements against the current published rate card.

Vague disputes — “this fee seems too high” without a specific measurement to counter it — get bounced back for more information almost every time. A dispute that leads with a photographed measurement and a specific corrected figure gives Seller Support something concrete to check rather than something to interpret.

The case you should not file: when the audit finds Amazon underbilled you

In one account we audited, a client wanted to dispute what looked like an overcharged fulfillment fee across a batch of SKUs. Running the numbers before filing, the audit found the opposite on nine of those products: Amazon was actually undercharging relative to their real shipped dimensions. We advised against filing. A remeasurement dispute reopens the number in both directions — filing it on those nine products would very likely have triggered a correction that raised the fee rather than lowered it. Knowing when not to file is a genuine, underappreciated part of fee work, and almost nothing published about Amazon fees mentions that a dispute can make your number worse, not better.

A worked example of a dispute that should win

Say your fee report shows a unit billed at a 2 lb dimensional weight, and your own measurement of the shipped package — 5.5 × 5.2 × 5.0 inches — comes to 143 cubic inches, which at the current 139 divisor works out to 1.03 lb, well under the 2 lb Amazon is charging. That's a genuine, checkable discrepancy: either Amazon's system is measuring an older or different package configuration, or a data error occurred somewhere in the catalog. Filed with the photographed measurements and the arithmetic shown, this is a strong dispute — the gap isn't a rounding disagreement, it's nearly double the correct figure, and the evidence is specific enough for Seller Support to check quickly rather than needing to interpret.

What to do when a filed dispute comes back denied

If the denial states Amazon remeasured and confirmed the existing fee, ask specifically whether the remeasurement used a package you actually shipped or a system record that may predate a packaging change on your end — this distinction matters because your product's packaging today may not be what Amazon's system last measured. If the remeasurement genuinely reflects your current package accurately, the fix isn't a second dispute with the same evidence; it's adjusting the box itself, which is the only lever that actually changes the number when the measurement was correct. Filing repeated disputes against an accurate measurement doesn't move the fee — it just adds noise to a case history that Amazon can see.

If the denial reason doesn't clearly address the evidence you submitted — a generic response that doesn't reference your photographed measurements at all — request a specific explanation before resubmitting anything. A second filing with identical evidence against an unstated objection rarely produces a different outcome; the more effective move is understanding exactly what the first review actually checked before deciding whether a follow-up is warranted.

Running this as an honest, two-way check

The discipline that makes fee disputes worth running is the same one that makes reimbursement claims worth running: check both directions before filing, file with specific evidence rather than a general complaint, and accept the answer when a remeasurement is accurate even if it isn't the one you wanted. Which one you should actually pick

A dispute backed by real photographed measurements and specific arithmetic is worth filing when the evidence genuinely supports a lower fee. A dispute filed on a hunch, without checking whether the reverse might be true, risks raising the fee it was meant to lower — the honest audit runs in both directions before either files or stays silent.

Common questions

Can I dispute an Amazon fulfillment fee I think is wrong?

Yes. File a case with Amazon Seller Support showing your own measurements of the shipped, sealed package — photos with a tape measure and a scale reading are the strongest evidence — and the current fee versus what your measurements support.

Can a fee dispute make my fulfillment fee go up instead of down?

Yes. A dispute triggers a remeasurement, and a remeasurement corrects the fee in whichever direction the real evidence points, including upward if the packaging genuinely grew or the original measurement was too low.

What evidence works best for an Amazon fee dispute?

Photographs of the actual shipped package against a tape measure on all three dimensions, a scale reading of the full shipped weight, and a specific corrected size tier calculated from those numbers — not a general statement that the fee seems too high.

Should I audit my own fees before disputing one?

Yes, and in both directions. An audit sometimes finds Amazon is undercharging on some SKUs even while overcharging on others — filing a dispute on the undercharged ones can raise their fee, so check before filing rather than after.

What if my fee dispute gets denied?

Ask whether the remeasurement used your current shipped package or an older system record. If the measurement is accurate, the fix is changing the packaging, not filing a repeat dispute with the same evidence against an accurate number.
