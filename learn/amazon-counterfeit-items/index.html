What counts as a counterfeit item on Amazon

A counterfeit is a product that copies a brand's name, logo or packaging without the brand's permission and is sold as if it were the real thing. That is different from a few other problems that get lumped in with it, and the difference decides where you report it.

  • Counterfeit: a fake made to pass as the genuine product, using the brand's trademark without permission.
  • Unauthorized or gray-market resale: a genuine product sold by someone the brand did not approve. The item is real, but the channel is not.
  • Listing hijack: a seller attaches an offer to an existing listing, so a shopper who thinks they are buying from the brand receives something else.
  • Look-alike or knock-off: a product that imitates the design but carries its own branding. That is a different legal question from counterfeiting.

For a shopper the practical result is the same: you get something that is not what you paid for. For a brand owner the distinction matters, because a trademark report only fits the first kind, while the others may need a different route.

How shoppers can spot a fake before and after buying

No single sign proves a product is fake, but several together raise the odds. Run this checklist before you order, especially for items in categories that get copied often: supplements, beauty, electronics accessories, cookware and baby products.

  • Check who is selling. On the product page, look at the line that says who ships and who sells. Buying from the brand's own storefront, or from a seller the brand names on its own website, is the safest route.
  • Compare the price. A price far below the brand's own website or other major retailers deserves a second look.
  • Read the listing closely. Misspelled brand names, mismatched images, odd grammar and missing ingredient or specification details are common in fake listings.
  • Read recent reviews for the wrong things. Look for shoppers who say the item looks different from the brand's packaging, smells or tastes different, or arrived without a seal or batch code. Weigh recent reviews more than old ones.
  • Check the brand's website. Many brands publish a list of authorized sellers and photos of genuine packaging.

After delivery, compare the item against the brand's own photos: packaging print quality, seals, batch codes, the feel of the material and any safety markings. For anything you swallow, apply to skin or give to a child, stop using it if something looks wrong.

How to report a counterfeit item as a shopper

Amazon gives shoppers a few routes, and it is worth using more than one if you bought something you think is fake. Menu names change, so check Amazon's own help pages for the current wording.

  • Report on the product page. Product pages carry a link to report an issue with the listing or the seller. Use it to flag a suspected counterfeit.
  • Contact the seller through your order. Ask for proof of authenticity, such as an invoice from an authorized supplier. A genuine seller can answer; silence or a vague reply is useful information.
  • Ask for a refund or open a claim. If the item is not what was described, request a return through your orders page, or use Amazon's A-to-z Guarantee for items sold by third-party sellers.
  • Tell the brand. The brand owner is the one with the power to act on a trademark. Most brands have a contact form for exactly this.

Keep photos of the item, the packaging and the order page. A report with pictures and a clear description of what differs from the genuine product is easier for anyone to act on than a one-line complaint.

What Amazon does about counterfeits, and what it leaves to brands

Amazon says it invests in detecting and blocking counterfeit listings, and it runs programs aimed at brand owners, including Brand Registry, product serialization through Transparency, and its Counterfeit Crimes Unit for pursuing bad actors. Check Amazon's own pages for the current scope and eligibility of each program, because they are revised over time.

The important point for readers is where the work sits. Amazon's tools depend heavily on the brand owner telling it what is genuine and what is not. A shopper can report a listing, but only the rights owner can say with authority that a given offer infringes its trademark. That is why a brand that is not enrolled in Brand Registry, or is enrolled but not watching its listings, tends to find out about a fake from a customer review rather than from its own monitoring.

It also means counterfeit handling is not a one-off clean-up. New offers can appear on an existing listing at any time, so the useful habit is a regular check rather than a reaction after a complaint.

What brand owners and sellers can do

If you own a trademark and sell on Amazon, the order of work is straightforward.

  • Register the trademark and enrol in Brand Registry. This gives you access to the tools for reporting infringement and more control over your listing content.
  • Watch your own listings. Review the sellers attached to each ASIN, the Buy Box and the new reviews on a set schedule.
  • Test-buy when something looks off. A purchase from a suspicious offer gives you the evidence to show what differs from the genuine product.
  • Report with evidence. Attach the trademark registration, the listing link, and photos showing how the item differs. State the facts and leave out opinion.
  • Use serialization or authenticity programs where they fit your category. Amazon's own pages list which ones are open to you.

One signal worth tracking is the Buy Box. Across 393 ASINs on 19 brands we manage, the brand did not hold the Buy Box in 24.9% of snapshots over 30 days (verified, Full Circle master database, 21 August 2026). A lost Buy Box has many ordinary causes, such as price, stock or an authorized reseller, so it is not proof of counterfeiting. It is, though, a useful trigger to look at who is winning the offer and whether you recognise them.

Reviews belong in this routine only for what they tell you. If shoppers say the item differs from your genuine product, treat that as a lead to investigate. Amazon accepts reports on reviews that break its community guidelines, and a review that is merely negative about a product is not removable, so keep the two separate.

Common questions

How can I tell if an Amazon item is counterfeit?

Check who is selling it, compare the price with the brand's own website, read the listing for spelling and image problems, and look at recent reviews for comments about packaging or quality. No single sign is proof, but several together are a good reason to buy from the brand's own storefront instead.

How do I report a counterfeit product on Amazon?

Use the report link on the product page, contact the seller through your order, and ask for a refund or open a claim if the item is not as described. Keep photos and the order number. If you are the brand owner, report through Brand Registry with your trademark details.

Does Amazon refund counterfeit items?

If an item is not what was described, you can request a return or refund through your orders page, and Amazon's A-to-z Guarantee covers items sold by third-party sellers. Eligibility and timing are set by Amazon, so check its current help pages.

Is a lost Buy Box a sign that someone is selling fakes?

Not by itself. Price, stock and authorized resellers all affect the Buy Box. It is a prompt to look at who is winning the offer on your ASIN. In our own data across 393 ASINs on 19 brands, the brand did not hold the Buy Box in 24.9% of snapshots over 30 days.

Can a negative review be removed because it says my product is fake?

Only if it breaks Amazon's community guidelines. A review that is simply negative about the product is not removable. A review saying the item differs from your genuine product is better treated as a lead: investigate the offer and report the listing with evidence.
