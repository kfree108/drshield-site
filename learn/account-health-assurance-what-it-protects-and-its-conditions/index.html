What AHA actually protects against

The core protection is straightforward: for most policy violations that would otherwise trigger an account deactivation, an enrolled seller instead gets proactive outreach from Amazon's own account health team, explaining the issue and what's needed to resolve it. As long as the seller engages within 72 hours of that outreach and completes the required corrective action, the account stays active throughout the process rather than going dark while the issue is sorted out.

This matters because deactivation itself is often the most expensive part of a violation — not the underlying issue, but the total loss of sales while an appeal works its way through review. AHA is specifically designed to decouple those two things: the violation still needs fixing, but the account doesn't have to go offline while that happens, which is the actual commercial risk most sellers are trying to avoid.

The eligibility conditions, precisely

Amazon's own stated criteria, from its account health guidance: an Account Health Rating of 250 or higher maintained for at least six months, with no more than 10 days total below that line during the period; a Professional selling plan; and a valid emergency contact number kept current. Ongoing participation additionally requires engaging with the account health team within 72 hours of any notification that they need to work with you, keeping the AHR from falling below 250 for more than 10 consecutive days, meeting regulatory compliance obligations, fully resolving issues raised, and avoiding repeated policy violations.

These are meaningfully stricter than the general 200-point healthy band described elsewhere on the Account Health dashboard — AHA sits on top of an already-healthy account, not a merely adequate one, which is why not every seller who feels their account is in reasonably good shape actually qualifies.

Worth noting too: none of these conditions are one-time gates you clear and forget. They're maintained continuously, which means an account can qualify for AHA one quarter and lose eligibility the next if its rating slips or a contact detail goes stale — checking your own status periodically, not just assuming it once confirmed, is part of keeping the protection in place.

What happens if you don't respond within the window

The 72-hour engagement window is the operative condition, not a soft suggestion. Missing it on a specific notification doesn't necessarily mean instant, permanent loss of the program, but it does mean the specific protection tied to that notification may lapse, and the underlying violation proceeds through Amazon's ordinary deactivation and appeal process instead — the exact outcome AHA exists to prevent. For a seller managing several ASINs and a full support inbox, a 72-hour clock that starts the moment Amazon reaches out, not the moment it's noticed, is a real operational risk if outreach isn't being watched for actively.

There's also a compounding risk worth naming: the ongoing eligibility conditions include avoiding repeated policy violations and fully resolving issues raised. A missed window on one notification doesn't just risk that single case — depending on how it's handled afterward, it can also put continued AHA participation itself at risk if it reads as a pattern rather than a one-time lapse.

What AHA does not cover

AHA is not blanket immunity, and treating it that way is the most common misunderstanding. It doesn't protect against every violation category — Amazon's most serious classifications, including counterfeit, payment fraud, identity fraud, and child-safety violations, sit outside standard AHA protection, both as ongoing conditions and, for some, as prior-history disqualifiers at enrollment. It also isn't a substitute for actually fixing the underlying issue — it buys time and keeps the account live while the fix happens, but the corrective action still has to be real, complete, and delivered inside the window.

It's worth being honest with yourself about which category a given issue falls into before assuming AHA has you covered. A seller who assumes every notification is routine, because AHA has always worked before, is exactly the seller most at risk of missing the one notification that falls outside the program's scope entirely and needs a different, faster response.

How to build engagement monitoring so the window never gets missed

Because the clock starts on Amazon's contact, not on your discovery of it, the practical defense is monitoring every plausible channel — the Account Health dashboard itself, the registered account email, and the emergency contact number — on a short, consistent cycle, not just when something already feels wrong. A single missed notification during a slow week is exactly the failure mode that a busy season, a staff transition, or a simple inbox filter misconfiguration can quietly cause.

Treat any account-health outreach as the highest-priority item in the inbox on the day it lands, ahead of routine catalog work, precisely because the response window is fixed and unforgiving in a way most other Amazon deadlines are not.

For a small team, that often means a named backup person who also has visibility into the account, not just the primary owner — a 72-hour window that falls entirely on one person's attention during a vacation or an illness is a genuine, avoidable gap. Assigning that backup explicitly, before it's needed, is a five-minute task that closes a real hole.

Where this fits into broader account-health casework

AHA changes the stakes of a policy violation, but it doesn't change the underlying work: gathering the right evidence, responding accurately, and documenting the exchange. Across the 53 accounts we've run casework on this year, the ones that get the most value from AHA are the ones that already have someone watching for account-health outreach daily, not weekly, and who treat the corrective-action requirement as seriously as the deadline itself — a rushed, incomplete response inside the window is not meaningfully better than a complete one delivered late, since both risk the same underlying deactivation.

Which one you should actually pick

AHA suits a seller who already maintains a genuinely strong account — AHR consistently above 250, Professional plan, current contact details — and wants the deactivation risk removed from an otherwise-solid operation. It doesn't suit an account with a weaker or inconsistent rating, and it never replaces actually fixing the underlying violation inside the response window.

Common questions

What is Amazon Account Health Assurance?

A free program that keeps an eligible seller's account active through most policy-violation deactivation triggers, provided the seller engages with Amazon's account health team within 72 hours of being contacted and completes the requested corrective action.

Who is eligible for Account Health Assurance?

Sellers on a Professional plan with an Account Health Rating of 250 or above maintained for at least six months (no more than 10 days total below that line) and a valid emergency contact number on file. Enrollment is automatic once eligible.

What happens if I miss the 72-hour Account Health Assurance window?

The specific protection tied to that notification can lapse, and the violation proceeds through Amazon's ordinary deactivation and appeal process instead — which is exactly the outcome AHA is meant to prevent, so missing the window has real consequences.

Does Account Health Assurance cover every type of violation?

No. Amazon's most serious violation categories — counterfeit, payment fraud, identity fraud, and child-safety issues — sit outside standard AHA protection, both as ongoing conditions and, in some cases, as disqualifiers based on prior account history.

Do I need to apply for Account Health Assurance?

No. Enrollment is automatic once an account meets the eligibility conditions, and Amazon sends a confirmation when it happens. There's no separate application to submit.

Can I lose Account Health Assurance after enrolling?

Yes. Ongoing participation depends on keeping the Account Health Rating from falling below 250 for more than 10 consecutive days, maintaining current contact information, and avoiding repeated policy violations — it isn't a one-time qualification that's permanent regardless of later account behavior.
