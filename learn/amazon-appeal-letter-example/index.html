The rejected draft

A composite example, built to show the mechanism, not drawn from any real account: "We were surprised to receive this notice as we take compliance very seriously. We believe this may have resulted from an issue with one of our suppliers. We have spoken with our team about being more careful with our listings going forward and are confident this won't happen again. We respectfully ask that our listing be reinstated." This reads as sincere and reasonably professional — and it still gets rejected, because it contains zero checkable facts.

Read it again as a reviewer would, scanning specifically for three things: a confirmed cause, a completed action, and a named ongoing control. None of the three is actually present, even though the paragraph is well-organized and grammatically clean. That gap between how a draft reads to its own author and how it reads to a reviewer checking for specific content is exactly what this walkthrough exists to close.

Line by line, why it fails

"We were surprised to receive this notice" — persuasive framing, tells the reviewer nothing about the case. "We believe this may have resulted from an issue with one of our suppliers" — a guess, not a confirmed root cause; "may have" signals the diagnosis wasn't actually completed. "We have spoken with our team about being more careful" — a conversation isn't a process; there's no named control, no date, nothing a reviewer could verify happened. "We are confident this won't happen again" — a sentiment, not a preventive action. "We respectfully ask that our listing be reinstated" — persuasion aimed at the reviewer's discretion rather than evidence aimed at the actual review criteria.

Notice that every one of these five sentences is individually reasonable as something a genuinely responsible seller might feel and say. The problem isn't dishonesty or laziness — it's that none of them, however sincere, gives a reviewer a single fact to check against the notice's own specifics.

Keep both versions side by side while drafting your own case — the rejected version as a checklist of phrasing to avoid, the fixed version as a model for how much specificity each sentence needs to carry.

The fixed version

"On [date], we identified that [named supplier] shipped a packaging variant inconsistent with our approved listing content between [date range], affecting [specific quantity] units across [specific ASINs]. We removed the affected inventory from sale on [date] and issued refunds to the [number] customers who received the affected packaging, confirmation attached. As of [date], we require [named supplier] to notify us in writing of any packaging change before shipment, verified by [role/person] against current listing content before any new inventory is released for sale." Every sentence in the fixed version names a fact a reviewer could, in principle, check — dates, a named supplier, a specific quantity, a completed action, and an ongoing control with an owner.

Notice too what the fixed version doesn't do: it doesn't argue that the violation was unfair, doesn't apologize at length, and doesn't ask the reviewer to reconsider. It simply states what happened, what was done, and what's now in place — trusting that a reviewer checking those three things against the notice will draw their own conclusion once the facts are on the page.

Read the fixed version once more with an eye specifically for what's absent — no adjectives describing effort or intent, only facts a stranger could check against a record somewhere.

What actually changed, structurally

The word count barely moved between the two versions — the fix isn't about writing more, it's about replacing vague, persuasive language with specific, checkable facts in the same three slots (root cause, corrective action, preventive action) the rejected draft was already loosely organized around. This is the single most transferable lesson across every case type: the structure in the rejected draft wasn't wrong, the content inside it was too vague to verify. Fixing a rejected plan of action is usually a specificity edit, not a rewrite from scratch.

This composite example is built to illustrate the mechanism, not drawn from any specific seller's account — the dates, product type, and supplier details are placeholders. What's real and directly transferable is the structural lesson, which applies whether the actual violation involves packaging, a listing error, or almost any other policy issue with a similar shape.

The common mistake this example illustrates

The rejected draft is a realistic first attempt — it's not lazy or careless, it's what most sellers write under the stress of a fresh suspension, before the actual root cause has been confirmed against real records. That's the pattern worth internalizing: a first draft written from memory and good intentions, before diagnosis is complete, tends to produce exactly this shape. The fix isn't better writing skill; it's completing the diagnosis first, so the facts exist to write down.

This is worth saying plainly because the natural response to seeing a rejected draft next to a fixed one is to conclude the seller who wrote the rejected version did something wrong. Usually they didn't — they wrote what they knew at the time, which was less than what the fixed version required. The gap is informational, not a character flaw, and it closes with research, not talent.

Why running this exercise on your own draft helps

Before submitting any plan of action, run this same test against your own draft: underline every sentence that names a specific date, a specific fact, or a specific completed action. If a sentence isn't underlined, it's very likely the kind of language that reads as persuasion rather than evidence — and worth cutting or replacing. Which one you should actually pick

The gap between a rejected plan and an accepted one is rarely length or sincerity — it's whether every sentence names something a reviewer could actually check. Run that test on your own draft before submitting, not after a rejection points it out. Read the notice twice before drafting anything.

Common questions

Why did a well-written, sincere plan of action still get rejected?

Because sincerity and clear writing don't substitute for specific, checkable facts — dates, named causes, completed actions. A reviewer is checking whether the plan demonstrates and proves understanding and correction, not whether it reads as genuine.

How do I know if my draft has the same problem as the rejected example?

Underline every sentence in your draft that names a specific date, fact, or completed action. If most of your draft isn't underlined, it likely reads as persuasive rather than evidentiary — the same gap the rejected example has.

Does fixing a rejected plan mean starting over?

Usually not. The structure is often already close to right — the fix is typically replacing vague statements with specific facts inside the same three sections, not rewriting the whole submission from scratch.

Is a longer plan of action more likely to be specific?

Not necessarily — the fixed example above is barely longer than the rejected one. Length doesn't create specificity; naming actual dates, causes, and completed actions does.
