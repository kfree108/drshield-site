The short answer

Every Amazon Plan of Action follows the same three-part skeleton: root cause (what specifically happened, when, and why), correction (what's already been fixed, with evidence), and prevention (a specific, named process change). What changes by violation type is the content inside each section, not the structure — shown below across a few common cases.

The skeleton, labeled

Root Cause: [Name the specific event, process failure, or error — with dates and, where relevant, an order or ASIN count. Avoid describing the violation category in general terms; describe what actually happened in your operation.]

Corrective Action: [Describe what has already been done to fix the specific instance — not what will be done. Attach evidence: a screenshot, a document, a tracking confirmation.]

Preventive Action: [Describe the new process, checkpoint, or safeguard now in place, naming who owns it and how it works. The test: could the same root cause still happen under this new process? If yes, it's not specific enough yet.]

Label each section explicitly with these headings in the actual submission — a reviewer moving through volume scans for these three specific elements, and clear labeling makes the document faster and more reliably read than the same content woven into unstructured paragraphs.

How it fills for a listing-content violation

Root cause: 'A bullet point on ASIN [X] contained a claim not supported by our current labeling, introduced during a bulk content update on [date].' Correction: 'The bullet point was corrected on [date]; screenshot of current listing attached.' Prevention: 'Bulk content updates are now reviewed by a second team member against current labeling before publishing, starting [date].'

How it fills for an authenticity complaint

Root cause: 'A single unit sourced from [specific distributor] on [date] did not match our standard supply chain due to [specific reason].' Correction: 'The affected inventory was identified and removed from sale on [date]; invoices for our standard, verified supply chain are attached.' Prevention: 'All new distributor relationships are now verified against our authorized-supplier list before any inventory is received, starting [date].'

How it fills for an account-health metric violation

Root cause: 'Our [specific metric] exceeded Amazon's threshold during [specific window] due to [specific operational cause].' Correction: 'The specific instances have been resolved; [evidence — tracking numbers, refund confirmations, etc.] attached.' Prevention: '[Specific new process, alert, or staffing change], owned by [role/person], now in place starting [date].'

What to change before submitting, every time

Replace every bracketed placeholder with your actual, specific facts — a submission that still reads like a template, with generic language standing in for real detail, is the single most common reason an otherwise well-structured POA gets denied. The structure above is reusable; the content inside it has to be genuinely yours, tied to your specific case, or the structure alone won't carry it.

Read your finished draft once specifically looking for any sentence that could apply to a different seller's different case without changes — if a sentence would work equally well in someone else's POA for a similar violation, it's still too generic and needs a more specific replacement tied to your actual facts.

When the template alone isn't enough

A structurally correct but evidence-thin POA — every section filled, but without the actual documents attached or specific enough detail — often reads as complete to the person writing it and incomplete to the reviewer reading it, which is why an outside read before submission catches gaps the writer can't see in their own draft. Common questions

Is there a standard template for an Amazon Plan of Action?

The structure is standard — root cause, corrective action, preventive action — but the content inside each section has to be specific to your actual case. A generic, unfilled-in template reads as incomplete regardless of its structure.

What's the most common mistake when using a Plan of Action template?

Leaving language generic where it should be specific — describing the violation category instead of the actual event, or promising a fix instead of documenting one already made. The structure is reusable; the specificity has to be genuine.

Does the same POA template work for every violation type?

The three-part skeleton works for every type, but what belongs inside each section differs — an authenticity complaint needs different evidence than a listing-content or account-health violation, even though the structure is identical.

Should I have someone else review my POA before submitting?

It helps. A POA that reads as complete to its own writer often has gaps a fresh reader catches — generic language mistaken for specific detail is hard to see in your own draft.
