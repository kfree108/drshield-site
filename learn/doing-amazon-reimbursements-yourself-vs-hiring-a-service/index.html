Doing Amazon Reimbursements Yourself vs Hiring a Service

Updated November 4, 2026 · By Ken Freeman · 1486 words · Written against what currently ranked for “doing Amazon reimbursements yourself vs hiring a service”
The short answer

Doing Amazon FBA reimbursements yourself costs no fees, only the hours to reconcile reports and file inside each claim window every week. A contingency service takes a share of what it recovers and runs that routine for you. Choose by catalog size and whether you will keep the weekly habit going.

What DIY actually requires, week to week

Filing your own claims is not technically hard. It is a report-reconciliation habit. You pull your inventory adjustment report, your FBA returns report and your removal shipment status, cross-reference them against what Amazon has already reimbursed, and open cases for the genuine gaps inside their windows.

The windows are what make it a weekly job. Per Amazon's reimbursement policy page, fulfillment-center losses and removal shortfalls run 60 days from the event, removals lost in transit run 15 to 75 days from shipment, and customer returns that never came back cannot be filed until 60 days after the refund, closing at 120. A quarterly review misses claims in at least one of those categories almost by definition, because the eligible window opens and closes between checks.

The real cost of DIY is not the form. It is doing this every week, indefinitely, on top of sourcing, advertising and support.

What a service actually buys you

A reimbursement service does not hold secret policy knowledge. The policy is public, and a diligent seller can read the same pages we do. What you buy is a standing cadence: someone, or some system, runs the reconciliation every week whether or not anyone remembered, and builds the evidence trail (sourcing invoices, shipment IDs, removal confirmations) before a deadline forces it.

It does not automatically buy a higher approval rate on any one claim. The evidence standard is the same either way. The advantage is coverage and consistency, not a trick for winning individual cases.

A good service should also buy restraint. Amazon can treat bulk, premature or under-researched filing as grounds for account monitoring, so ask any provider how it decides what not to file.

Where the money actually comes from

It helps to know which claim types are worth the habit.   • Customer service issues: $102,858 (4,202 items)
  • Damaged in warehouse: $83,426 (5,594 items)

Two things follow for a DIY filer. First, the largest category by value, customer returns never returned, is also the one with the longest wait (60 days before you can file, 120 to close), so it is the easiest to forget. Second, gross is not what you keep: Amazon reverses some reimbursements, so track net, not just claims paid.

A worked breakeven you can rerun with your own numbers

The comparison is simple: the fee a service would take against the value of the hours DIY would take. Here is a worked example with made-up inputs, not a result from any account.

  • For example, say a proper weekly check recovers $4,000 a month for your catalog.
  • If a service took 20% of recoveries, that is $800 a month.
  • If DIY takes 5 hours a month and your time is worth $60 an hour, that is $300 a month.

On those inputs DIY is cheaper, provided the five hours really happen every month. Change one input and the answer moves. If recoveries are $12,000 a month at the same 20%, the fee is $2,400, and DIY still wins on cash but only if the hours do not grow with the claim volume, which they usually do.

The number that decides most real cases is not on this list: it is the share of weeks the process actually runs. A process that runs three weeks in four recovers a fraction of one that runs all four, so a cheaper plan that lapses can cost more than a fee.

When the calculation changes

With a small catalog and modest loss volume, DIY is usually the more efficient choice. The reconciliation is small enough to run as a Friday-morning habit, and a percentage fee on a small recovery base is not worth the convenience.

As catalog size and claim volume grow, the weekly reconciliation stops being a habit and becomes close to a part-time job. That is when a fee starts buying real hours back. There is no universal SKU count where this flips, because loss rate per SKU varies by category and packaging. The honest signal is whether the reconciliation is still happening every week, on time, three months in. If it is not, the DIY maths was theoretical.

A quick self-test:

  • Did you file or review claims in each of the last 8 weeks?
  • Can you say which claims are still inside their windows today?
  • Do you know your net recovered figure after reversals?

If you cannot answer yes to all three, a service may pay for itself on consistency alone.

What to do when DIY reimbursements have already lapsed

If a self-run process has gone quiet for six weeks or more, start with triage, not panic. Pull the last three months of refund, removal and inventory-adjustment reports and check what is still inside its window. Customer-return claims older than four months are likely past the 120-day close. Fulfillment-center losses from the last two months may still be fileable.

This is also the moment most sellers really decide between DIY and a service: not at a theoretical breakeven, but after a lapse has cost money that cannot be recovered. That is a legitimate way to decide. Ask what actually broke: time, attention or genuine complexity. Only the first two are solved by paying someone else to run it.

Neither answer is universally right

A seller who runs the weekly check, keeps sourcing invoices organized and has time to spare is well served in-house. There is no fee to pay, and a service adds no case-craft advantage on top of a disciplined internal process. A seller whose time is better spent on sourcing or advertising, or whose reconciliation has already slipped once, gets more from paying a percentage for continuity than from a free process that runs inconsistently.

Which one you should actually pick

Small catalog, real discipline, time to spare: do it yourself and do not pay a fee for something you already do well. Growing catalog, stretched team, or a process that has slipped once: a contingency service buys back consistency, which is what actually drives recovery, not access to information you could not find yourself.

What to do with this

Shortlist on the job, not the feature grid. Ask each provider to walk you through one real case from start to finish: the evidence they attached, what Amazon said, and what happened when the first answer was no. See who answers with a process rather than a screenshot.

Common questions

Is it worth filing Amazon reimbursement claims myself?

For a smaller catalog with modest claim volume and a real weekly reconciliation habit, yes. The policy is public and the process, while tedious, is not complex. The risk is consistency: a process that runs three weeks in four recovers far less than one that runs every week.

How much time does DIY reimbursement filing take?

It depends on catalog size and loss rate. A realistic weekly reconciliation, covering pulling reports, cross-referencing and filing cases inside the right windows, takes several hours a week for a catalog with meaningful claim volume, and it never ends. Time your first month and use that figure in the breakeven above.

At what point does a reimbursement service become worth the fee?

When the weekly habit is realistically not going to hold, or when the hours are worth more elsewhere in the business than the fee saved. There is no fixed SKU threshold, because it depends on loss rate and how consistently the process runs.

Can I recover claims from a process I let lapse?

Partly. Claim windows run from 15 to 120 days depending on the type, according to Amazon's reimbursement policy page, so recent losses may still be fileable while older ones are likely gone. Triage what is still open rather than rebuilding everything.

Does a reimbursement service win more claims than I would filing myself?

Not on the strength of the evidence, since Amazon applies the same standard either way. What a service adds is consistency and coverage, the part that tends to slip when it is self-managed.
