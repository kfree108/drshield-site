How Amazon Reimburses Lost Inventory Now: Manufacturing Cost, Not Sale Price

Updated November 4, 2026 · By Ken Freeman · 1391 words · Written against what currently ranked for “how Amazon reimburses lost inventory now: manufacturing cost, not sale price”
The short answer

Since 31 March 2025, Amazon reimburses inventory lost or damaged before it reaches a customer at your documented sourcing cost, which is what you paid your manufacturer or wholesaler, not the price it would have sold for. Without your documentation, Amazon substitutes its own cost estimate.

The valuation rule, stated precisely

According to Amazon's FBA inventory reimbursement policy, losses fall into two buckets, and each is valued differently. A pre-order loss is a unit lost, damaged or destroyed inside Amazon's network before any customer bought it. It reimburses at your sourcing cost: the documented amount you paid to acquire the unit, excluding freight and duties. A post-order loss, most commonly a customer return that vanishes before it is checked back in, reimburses at the refund amount minus the fees that applied to that sale. That is much closer to what most sellers picture when they hear “reimbursement.”

The distinction matters because many articles still describe reimbursement as a single number. If you forecast recovery at retail price across your whole loss ledger, you will overestimate the pre-order half of it.

Why the change matters for your forecast

Reimbursement is meant to make you whole for what you lost, which is the cost of the unit, not the margin you would have earned by selling it. Under the sourcing-cost basis, a pre-order claim pays back what you spent. The gap between the old expectation and the new payout is your margin on that unit.

For most sellers the practical result is a forecasting change, not a policy to fight. Budget pre-order recovery against landed-cost-free unit cost, and budget post-order recovery against refund value less fees.

The worked example: same loss, two valuation bases

For example, take a unit that costs $9.40 to source and sells for $32.99. Lost inside a fulfillment center before any customer order touches it, that unit now reimburses at $9.40, the documented sourcing cost. If you had expected the $32.99 sale price, the payout is 71.5% smaller than your expectation ($32.99 minus $9.40, divided by $32.99). Multiply that gap across a catalog with real loss volume and it is the difference between a reimbursement line that offsets shrinkage and one that barely registers.

Now take the same $32.99 item as a customer return that is refunded and never comes back. That is a post-order loss. It reimburses closer to the $32.99 refund, minus the fees Amazon would have charged on the sale. Two losses on the identical SKU are valued on different bases purely because of when in the order lifecycle the unit went missing.

A quick way to run this on your own ledger:

  • Split your lost and damaged units into pre-order and post-order.
  • Value pre-order units at documented unit cost.
  • Value post-order units at refund amount less applicable fees.
  • Add the two. That total is your realistic recovery, not units times retail price.

What counts as documentation, and what doesn't

Amazon wants a record that ties your claimed cost to the specific unit or ASIN, typically a manufacturer or wholesaler invoice showing per-unit cost. If you submit nothing, Amazon substitutes its own cost estimate. Submissions can be declined for what the policy describes as outlier values, or documents that are “forged, tampered with, or illegible.” A blurry phone photo, or a spreadsheet total that does not map to the unit in question, is an avoidable reason for a claim to underpay.

A documentation checklist:

  • Keep supplier invoices as clear PDFs, not photos.
  • Show per-unit cost on the invoice, mapped to the SKU or ASIN.
  • Put freight and duties on their own lines, separate from unit cost, because the sourcing-cost definition excludes them.
  • For a multi-SKU shipment, include a per-unit cost allocation so each ASIN has a clean number.
  • Store invoices where your team can find them within minutes, before a claim deadline arrives.

What to do when the reimbursement lands lower than expected

First, check which bucket the loss fell into, pre-order or post-order. That alone explains most of the gap between expectation and payout. If it was a pre-order loss and you submitted no documentation, supply your own invoice on the next claim rather than accepting Amazon's category estimate by default. If you did submit documentation and the payout still looks wrong, ask for the valuation basis Amazon applied before resubmitting, because a second claim with the same paperwork gap will not produce a different number.

Also check the invoice for freight and duty bundled into the per-unit price. Amazon's sourcing-cost definition excludes shipping and duties, so a landed-cost figure that includes them can be trimmed in review even when the invoice is legitimate. That is the policy applied to a document that mixed two cost concepts. And remember that the policy has a per-unit cap (the FAQ below gives it), so a very high-cost item can be underpaid relative to its true cost even with perfect paperwork.

Lost inbound followed at $215,676 across 1,271 items, the kind of loss where unit cost and invoices matter most. The lesson is to look at where your own losses sit before budgeting recovery.

Who handles this best

If you have a small catalog, tidy invoices and the time to read your own Fulfillment by Amazon reports, filing these claims yourself is reasonable. The paperwork habits above will do most of the work. Common questions

Does Amazon reimburse at sale price or cost?

Cost, for anything lost or damaged before a customer bought it (a pre-order loss), effective 31 March 2025. A post-order loss, like a refunded return that never comes back, pays on the refund amount less applicable fees.

What is 'sourcing cost' in an Amazon reimbursement claim?

The documented amount you paid your manufacturer or wholesaler for the unit, excluding shipping and duties. Amazon uses your documentation if you provide it and its own estimate if you don't.

Why did my Amazon reimbursement pay less than I expected?

Two common reasons: the loss was pre-order and reimbursed at cost rather than sale price, or no sourcing documentation was submitted and Amazon used its own category estimate.

Is there a maximum Amazon will reimburse per unit?

Yes, $5,000 per unit under Amazon's policy, regardless of which valuation basis applies.

Can I dispute a reimbursement that used Amazon's cost estimate instead of mine?

Submit clear, unit-mapped documentation on the next claim rather than resending the same claim with the same gap. Keeping sourcing invoices organized before you need them is the more reliable habit.
