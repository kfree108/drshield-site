What the Case Log actually is

The Case Log is Seller Central's running record of every ticket you've opened or that's been opened on your account — a support request, a reimbursement claim, a suspension appeal, a fee dispute. Each gets a case ID, a status, and a running message thread with Amazon. It's not just a support inbox; for anything requiring evidence or follow-up, the Case Log is effectively the file Amazon's reviewers are working from, which makes how you use it part of how well a case gets resolved, not just where you check on it.

Every message you send inside a case becomes part of that case's permanent record, which cuts both ways: a clear, evidence-backed message strengthens the file every time you add one, while a frustrated "why hasn't this been resolved yet" message with no new information adds nothing useful and can read, across several such messages, as exactly the kind of repeated pressure Amazon's own guidance discourages.

The rule that matters most: one case ID per issue

The single highest-leverage habit in the Case Log is keeping exactly one case ID open per distinct issue. Opening a second case for the same problem — because the first felt slow, or a different support rep might respond faster — splits your evidence and your history across two threads that Amazon's reviewers may not connect, and it can read as the kind of duplicate, insufficiently researched submission Amazon's own policy specifically warns against. If a case genuinely needs escalation, the correct move is escalating that same case ID, not opening a fresh one and starting over.

This applies even when a case genuinely spans more than one issue at first glance — two related but distinct problems, like a reimbursement claim and a fee dispute on the same shipment, are usually still two separate case IDs, not one combined case or two duplicates of either.

A worked example of managing parallel cases

An account running normal volume might have three or four open cases simultaneously — a reimbursement claim for lost inventory, a fee dispute over a dimension remeasurement, and a listing suppression appeal. Managed well, each has its own case ID, a note of what evidence was submitted and when, and a follow-up date rather than being checked reactively when something reminds you. Managed poorly, the same three issues turn into six or seven case IDs because a slow response on one prompted a duplicate, and now the same reimbursement claim is competing with itself for review — a common, avoidable way a fast-moving business creates its own delays.

A simple external log — spreadsheet, ticketing tool, whatever's already in use — with columns for case ID, type, filed date, evidence attached, and next follow-up date turns this from a memory exercise into a checklist. That alone closes most of the gap between a well-run case log and a fragmented one.

Reading case status correctly

A case sitting in "Amazon is investigating" or an equivalent open status isn't necessarily stalled — response times vary by case type and volume, and Amazon doesn't publish a fixed SLA for most categories. What's worth tracking is elapsed time against your own reasonable expectation, not against a published deadline that doesn't exist. If a case has genuinely gone quiet well past what similar cases in your history have taken, following up on the existing case ID — with any new relevant information, not just "any update?" — is the right move, not opening a new case.

Status labels themselves are worth learning to read literally rather than emotionally. "Awaiting your response" means the ball is in your court and the case is not moving until you act — checking that status daily costs nothing and can save days on a case where a follow-up question sat unanswered. "Under review" means Amazon has what it needs and the delay is genuinely on their side; following up here adds noise without changing the timeline.

The common mistake: treating the log as a mailbox instead of a file

The mistake we see most is checking the Case Log reactively — only when a notification arrives — rather than maintaining it as a working file with next actions and dates. A reimbursement claim that needs a follow-up at day 14 if unresolved, or a fee dispute that needs a specific document attached that wasn't ready at filing time, both need someone tracking them forward, not waiting for Amazon to prompt the next step. On accounts running several cases at once, that tracking gap is usually where cases quietly expire past their filing window or lose momentum, not a decision Amazon made against the seller.

The arithmetic behind this is straightforward: a reimbursement claim with a 60-day filing window that sits unreviewed in the Case Log for 45 days before anyone checks it leaves 15 days to gather evidence and file correctly — often not enough time if the required documentation isn't already on hand. The same claim, checked weekly from the day the loss was identified, has the full window to work with. The Case Log doesn't enforce that discipline on its own; it just records whatever discipline you bring to it.

Why this discipline scales

One case is manageable from memory; five or ten running simultaneously across reimbursements, appeals, and fee disputes isn't. Which one you should actually pick

The Case Log rewards discipline more than urgency — one case ID per issue, tracked forward with dates rather than checked reactively. Most delays we see trace back to fragmented cases competing with themselves, not to Amazon being genuinely slow.

Common questions

Should I open a new case if my current one is taking too long?

No. Follow up on the existing case ID with new relevant information rather than opening a duplicate — a second case for the same issue splits your evidence and can read as the kind of duplicate submission Amazon's policy discourages.

How do I know if a case is actually stalled?

Amazon doesn't publish a fixed response time for most case types, so compare against your own account's typical resolution time for similar cases rather than a fixed number. Meaningfully longer than usual is worth a follow-up; a few extra days usually isn't.

Can I track multiple open cases in one place?

The Case Log itself lists every open and closed case with its ID and status, but it doesn't prompt follow-up actions — that tracking has to be maintained separately if you're running several cases at once.

What happens if I accidentally open a duplicate case?

Reference the original case ID in the duplicate and ask that it be merged or closed in favor of the first — leaving both open unattended is what actually causes confusion and delay, not the duplicate itself.

Should I keep my own record of cases outside Seller Central?

It helps considerably on accounts running several cases — a simple external tracker with case ID, filing date, window deadline, and next action catches expiring windows the Case Log itself won't proactively flag for you.
