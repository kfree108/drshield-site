The short answer

Amazon can hold seller funds for several genuinely different reasons — a routine reserve against future returns and claims, a disbursement pause during an active investigation, or a permanent withholding tied to a fraud finding — and each releases differently. A routine reserve clears on its own; the others need a specific case, or in the worst instance, legal escalation.

What Amazon's own agreement actually authorizes

Amazon's Business Solutions Agreement gives itself broad, explicit authority here. It can establish a reserve on an account, withholding amounts due, based on its own assessment of risk that a seller's actions or performance may result in returns, chargebacks, claims, or legal violations — and can modify that reserve at any time by notice. Separately, if Amazon determines an account has been used for deceptive, fraudulent, or illegal activity — or that making a payment would breach a law — it may, in its sole discretion, temporarily or permanently withhold any relevant payments. Those are two structurally different powers, invoked for two different reasons, and the case that releases the first rarely does anything for the second.

Reading the actual notice, when there is one, for which of these two powers it's citing is worth doing before assuming the worst. A reserve notice typically references risk on your account in general terms; an investigation or fraud-related hold typically references a specific concern, a specific case, or a specific ASIN. That difference in language is often the clearest early signal of which category you're actually dealing with.

Category one: the routine reserve

Most withheld funds at any given moment are a routine reserve — money held back against the real possibility of a return, an A-to-z claim, or a chargeback landing on an order that hasn't finished its return window yet. Amazon doesn't publish one universal formula for how this reserve is sized or how long it runs, and the honest thing to do is describe the structure rather than invent a figure: it's calculated against risk Amazon is currently carrying on your account, and it releases as that risk clears — as return windows lapse without a claim, and as your account's dispute rate stays low. This category doesn't usually need a case at all; it resolves on its own timeline as the underlying orders age out of their risk window.

Category two: a disbursement pause during an investigation

When Amazon becomes aware of activity it believes may not comply with its policies, disbursements can be paused while it investigates — a different, more serious category than the routine reserve, because it's tied to a specific concern rather than ordinary transactional risk. Amazon's own forum announcements describe this area as the Funds Disbursement Eligibility Policy, renamed from an earlier “Fund Withholding Policy” in an October 2024 update that also changed the appeal window from 90 days to 60 days after an account is deactivated. Sellers whose accounts remain deactivated can request a funds review after that window through Amazon's disbursement-appeals channel, submitting the documentation the investigation actually needs rather than a general argument that the hold is unfair.

Category three: a fraud finding, and where casework stops

The hardest category is a completed investigation that concludes the account was used for deceptive, fraudulent, or illegal activity. Under Amazon's own agreement, this is the one scenario where it can withhold payments permanently, at its sole discretion, with no contractual release mechanism spelled out the way there is for a routine reserve. This is also the point where the honest answer stops being “build a stronger appeal” and starts being the legal escalation we compared against our own casework — a demand letter, a formal dispute under the agreement, or arbitration are tools a case-management practice doesn't have and a law firm does. We say this plainly because pretending otherwise, past this point, wastes the time that legal escalation actually needs.

A case walk-through: a reserve, an investigation hold, and telling them apart

In one account, a sudden drop in available balance looked at first like a single event, and the instinct was to file one broad case asking Amazon to “release my funds.” Pulling the actual disbursement history showed two separate things stacked on top of each other: a routine reserve that had grown because return volume had genuinely ticked up that month, and a smaller, separate hold tied to an open investigation on one specific ASIN. Treating those as one problem would have meant the investigation case sat waiting on evidence nobody had gathered, while the reserve — which needed no case at all — got argued about anyway. Splitting them into two tracks, each with its own case ID where one applied, meant the reserve was left to clear on its own schedule while real effort went into the actual investigation, which is what needed a response. Running both together under one case would have achieved neither faster.

What to do while you wait, and what not to do

Don't let a withheld-funds case turn into daily follow-up messages asking for a status update — that adds noise to a queue without adding information, and in our experience makes a reviewer's job harder, not easier. Do keep the account itself clean while a hold is open: new policy flags, additional disputes, or a second investigation opened on top of the first only extend whatever timeline already exists. And do gather the specific documentation the hold is actually about — order records, correspondence, proof of the underlying transactions — before the case ever asks for it, since a reviewer moving faster on a complete file is the one lever genuinely available to you here.

Which one you should actually pick

A routine reserve is best left to clear on its own schedule — it rarely needs a case at all. A disbursement pause during an active investigation needs a specific, documented response through Amazon's disbursement-appeals channel. A confirmed fraud finding is where casework's usefulness ends and legal escalation genuinely begins — pretending a stronger appeal changes that outcome only spends time a lawyer could use instead.

Common questions

Why is Amazon holding my seller funds?

Usually a routine reserve against future returns, claims, or chargebacks on orders that haven't finished their return window. It can also be a disbursement pause during an active investigation, or, in the most serious cases, a permanent withholding tied to a fraud finding — each releases differently.

How long does a routine Amazon reserve last?

Amazon doesn't publish one fixed duration — it's sized and released against the actual risk on your account, easing as return windows lapse and disputes stay low. It generally clears on its own without needing a case filed.

How do I request a release of funds after my account was deactivated?

Amazon's Funds Disbursement Eligibility Policy allows a review request 60 days after deactivation, through its disbursement-appeals channel. Submit the specific documentation the underlying investigation needs, rather than a general argument that the hold is unfair.

Can Amazon withhold my funds permanently?

Yes — its own agreement allows permanent withholding, at its sole discretion, when an investigation concludes the account was used for deceptive, fraudulent, or illegal activity. This is the category where legal counsel, not another appeal, is usually the honest next step.

Should I keep messaging Amazon for updates while funds are withheld?

Repeated status-check messages tend to add noise without adding information. It's more productive to keep the account clean of new issues and gather the specific documentation the hold is actually about, ready for when the case asks for it.

What's the difference between a reserve and a disbursement pause?

A reserve is routine risk management against future returns, claims, or chargebacks, and generally clears on its own as that risk ages out. A disbursement pause is tied to a specific, active investigation into a compliance concern, and needs a documented response through the appeals channel rather than waiting it out.
