How this differs from a listing hijacker

A listing hijacker is an unauthorized seller on one of your ASINs — a catalog problem. A compromised or fraudulently enrolled Brand Registry account is a control problem: someone else has access to the systems that manage your entire brand's protections, or your trademark has been enrolled by a party who isn't you. The stakes are broader, because Brand Registry access controls the tools — Report a Violation, Transparency, Project Zero — that would otherwise be used to fix a narrower catalog issue.

Recognizing which situation you're actually in matters, because the recovery paths genuinely differ: a listing hijacker gets reported through Brand Registry; a compromised Brand Registry account itself needs a security-incident response, aimed at regaining control of the account before anything else.

A third, less common scenario worth naming: a former partner, agency, or employee who once had legitimate access retaining it after the relationship ended, and using it in a way the current brand owner didn't authorize. This sits between the two categories above — it's not a stranger's fraud, but it's also not a simple credential leak — and the recovery path is closer to the compromised-access scenario, since it starts with revoking access that should have been removed already.

Which of the three situations are you in? A quick triage

Answer these in order. The first "yes" tells you which path to start on.

  • Can you still log in, and do you see changes you did not make? Your credentials or a session have been used by someone else. Start with securing the account.
  • Can you no longer log in, or has the account's contact email or phone changed? Treat it as a takeover. Start with securing the account and the email address tied to it.
  • Does Brand Registry show your trademark enrolled under an account you have never heard of? That is an ownership dispute. Start with documentation proving you are the trademark owner.
  • Is the access held by someone you used to work with? That is the third scenario above. Start with removing their access, then review what they changed.

Write down the date and time you noticed the problem and what you saw. That first note becomes the opening line of your case record.

The first moves when access is compromised

If login credentials for the Brand Registry account are compromised — a shared password reused elsewhere, a former employee's access never revoked, a phishing incident — the immediate priority is securing the account: changing credentials, enabling or reviewing two-step verification, and reviewing any recent account activity for changes made without authorization. This is a security response first, before it's a Brand Registry case, because every minute the account stays accessible to someone else is a minute they can make further changes.

Check specifically whether any listings, Report a Violation submissions, or Transparency or Project Zero settings were touched during the compromised window — an unauthorized party with genuine Brand Registry access could make changes to any of these, and a full review of recent activity is the only reliable way to know what, if anything, was actually altered.

A working order for the first day:

  • Change the password on the Amazon login and on the email account that receives its codes.
  • Review and re-set two-step verification so the codes go to a device you hold.
  • List every user with access to Seller Central and Brand Registry, and remove anyone who does not need it today.
  • Screenshot the activity you can see before you change anything else, so the record shows what the account looked like when you found it.
  • Open one case with the Brand Registry team and note its case ID.

Proving ownership when your trademark is enrolled under someone else

If your brand appears enrolled in Brand Registry under an account you don't control — rather than your own access being compromised — the path is providing Amazon's Brand Registry team with documentation proving you're the actual trademark owner: registration certificates, the original registering party's details, and any evidence the enrollment wasn't authorized by you. This is a genuinely different, slower process than a standard Report a Violation case, because Amazon is being asked to resolve a dispute over who legitimately owns the underlying trademark.

Gather this documentation completely before the first submission rather than piecemeal afterward — a registration certificate alone is often not enough on its own to settle a dispute where someone else has already established some form of access, and a thin initial submission just adds a round trip to an already slower process.

A complete first submission usually holds:

  • The trademark registration certificate, with the registration number and the owner of record.
  • Proof that the account holder named on the certificate is you or your company, such as incorporation records or an assignment document.
  • Dated evidence of your use of the brand: packaging, product photos, invoices to your manufacturer.
  • A short written timeline of how and when you found the other enrollment, and a statement that you did not authorize it.
  • Any messages with a former agency, distributor or employee that bear on who was allowed to enroll the brand.

Check the current requirements on Amazon's Brand Registry page before you submit, since the rules for what counts as acceptable proof are Amazon's to change.

A first-week plan, day by day

Having an order for the first week keeps a stressful situation from turning into scattered, duplicate effort. Adjust it to your case, but keep the sequence.

  • Day 1: secure logins and email, remove unneeded users, screenshot everything, note the date and time you noticed. Open one Brand Registry case and save the case ID.
  • Day 2: pull your evidence pack together: trademark certificate, proof the owner of record is you, dated brand-use evidence and the written timeline.
  • Day 3: submit the complete pack in the one case, and add a short plain-language summary at the top saying which of the three situations applies.
  • Days 4 to 7: review every listing, Report a Violation submission and brand-protection setting for changes you did not make. Write down what you find, and add it to the same case rather than opening a new one.
  • After day 7: follow up on a steady rhythm, quoting the case ID each time, and ask for escalation to a specialist team if there has been no movement.

This is a working routine, not a promise about how fast Amazon will respond. How quickly the case moves is Amazon's decision, and an ownership dispute is checked more carefully than a routine violation report.

Why this needs urgency, not a routine case queue

A compromised or disputed Brand Registry enrollment isn't a case to let sit in a normal queue. Every day it's unresolved is a day the protections that keep counterfeiters, hijackers, and content changes off your listings are either unavailable to you or, worse, available to whoever has unauthorized access. Treat this the way you'd treat any serious account-security incident — immediate action, direct escalation where the channel allows it, and documentation of every step for the eventual case record.

This is also a case where the parallel-track approach genuinely applies: a Brand Registry-specific channel for the ownership or access question, alongside general seller support if the compromise has also affected listings or orders, cross-referenced to each other so neither team is working from a partial picture of what's actually happened.

What to do if the standard channel is slow to respond

Brand ownership disputes can move slowly precisely because Amazon is verifying a genuine ownership question rather than a simpler policy violation. If a submission with clear trademark documentation isn't producing movement, the case-management discipline still applies: keep one case ID, follow up on a regular cadence, and request escalation to a specialist team rather than repeatedly re-submitting the same documentation. For an especially stuck, high-stakes dispute, this is also a genuine point where legal counsel — someone who specializes in Amazon seller account and IP disputes — becomes a reasonable next step alongside the Amazon-side process, not instead of it.

Document every step of the recovery process as it happens — the date access was recovered, what changes were reviewed, what was found. That contemporaneous record is more credible later than a reconstruction written after the fact, whether it's needed for an internal review, a legal proceeding, or simply your own future reference.

Preventing this from happening again

Once control is recovered, the prevention work matters as much as the recovery: unique, non-reused credentials for the Brand Registry account, two-step verification enabled, access limited to people who currently need it and revoked promptly when they don't, and a documented record of who has access at any given time.

Across the seller accounts we've run casework on this year — casework that has recovered $803,457 from Amazon since February, net of reversals — the recovery cases that took longest were consistently ones where nobody could say with certainty who'd had access to the account historically — former employees, former agencies, a contractor whose access was never formally closed out. That uncertainty doesn't just slow a recovery; it makes proving the current, legitimate ownership harder in the first place, since there's no clean record to point to.

A simple access log fixes most of this. Keep a one-page list with a row per person or company: their name, what they can reach, the date access was granted, who approved it, and the date it was removed. Review it every quarter and whenever an agency or employee leaves.

Common questions

What's the difference between a listing hijacker and a Brand Registry compromise?

A listing hijacker is an unauthorized seller on one ASIN — a catalog problem reported through Brand Registry. A compromised Brand Registry account means someone else controls the tools that manage your whole brand's protections — a security problem that needs securing the account itself first.

How do I prove I own a trademark that's enrolled under the wrong Amazon account?

Provide Amazon's Brand Registry team with your trademark registration certificate, proof that you are the owner of record, dated evidence of your use of the brand, and a timeline showing the enrollment wasn't authorized by you. This is a genuine ownership dispute Amazon has to verify, so it moves more slowly than a standard violation report.

What should I do first if my Brand Registry login is compromised?

Secure the account immediately — change credentials, review or enable two-step verification, and check recent account activity for unauthorized changes — before anything else, since every additional minute of access is a risk. Then screenshot what you found and open one case.

Can I speed up a Brand Registry ownership dispute?

Not dramatically, since Amazon is verifying a genuine ownership question, but a complete first submission, one case ID, regular follow-up and a request for specialist escalation, rather than duplicate submissions, help avoid adding unnecessary delay.

A former agency still has access to my brand. What do I do?

Treat it like a compromised login: remove their user access in Seller Central and Brand Registry today, change shared passwords, then review what they changed. Keep any messages that show what they were and were not authorized to do, since they help if ownership is later disputed.

What should I check after regaining control of a Brand Registry account?

Review whether any listings, Report a Violation submissions, or Transparency and Project Zero settings were changed during the compromised window, and confirm current access is limited to people who actually still need it. Then start an access log so you can show who held access and when.
