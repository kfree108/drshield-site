Amazon Storage Fees and Stranded Inventory: the Compounding Problem

Updated November 5, 2026 · By Ken Freeman · 1748 words · Written against what currently ranked for “Amazon storage fees and stranded inventory: the compounding problem”
The short answer

Stranded inventory is stock inside an Amazon warehouse that cannot be sold because its listing has a problem. It keeps paying monthly storage fees the whole time, earns nothing, and moves closer to the aged-inventory surcharge each month. The fix is the listing, not the fee.

Why stranding is a fee problem, not just a listing problem

When a listing goes stranded (closed, deactivated, restricted, or stuck under review) the inventory behind it does not leave the warehouse. Amazon keeps charging monthly storage on it exactly as if it were sellable, because from a storage-billing standpoint it occupies the same cubic footage either way. A unit that would normally sell and clear its own storage cost through revenue instead sits generating a cost with zero offsetting income, for as long as the underlying listing problem stays unresolved.

That is the compounding part. This is not a fixed, one-off loss. It is an ongoing monthly charge that keeps accruing until someone fixes the reason the listing went stranded in the first place.

The second clock running underneath the first

Storage fees are the first cost. The aged-inventory surcharge is the second, and it is calendar-driven rather than event-driven: inventory that has been sitting past Amazon's current age threshold accrues an additional surcharge on top of standard storage, regardless of why it has been sitting. A unit that goes stranded and stays stranded is on both clocks at once. Stranding does not cause the aged surcharge directly, but it removes the one thing, a sale, that would have kept the unit from reaching the threshold.

The fourth quarter makes it worse. Storage rates step up for the holiday season, so a unit stranded since summer and still stranded in October moves from an off-peak rate to a peak-season rate, on top of any aged surcharge it has already picked up. Three cost pressures stack on a single unit that earns nothing. The same stranding that would have been a modest cost if resolved in August can be a meaningfully larger one by December, purely because of when it sat unresolved. Check Amazon's current FBA fee schedule on sell.amazon.com for the live rates and thresholds, since both are revised.

Every stranding reason is a listing problem

  • Listing closed: the ASIN itself was deactivated, often for a policy or compliance reason.
  • Listing error: a catalog data conflict, frequently from a category or attribute mismatch introduced by a recent edit.
  • Restricted ASIN: the product now requires category or brand approval that was not previously needed, or documentation has lapsed.
  • Under review: a compliance or authenticity flag is pending resolution.
  • Deleted: the listing was removed from the catalog entirely, sometimes by accident during a bulk update.

None of these is an inventory problem in the sense of too much stock or the wrong reorder timing. Every one traces back to something wrong with the listing sitting on top of the inventory. That is why the Stranded Inventory report in Seller Central has to be read alongside a listing-health check, not as a standalone inventory metric.

The point for a seller is the pattern, not the total: inventory problems are spread thinly across a normal portfolio, the way most fee leakage is, and they only show up if someone is looking at the reports on a regular cadence. Stranded stock and lost or damaged stock sit in the same FBA reports, so one monthly review can cover both.

A monthly stranded-inventory check you can run today

  • Total it: open the Stranded Inventory report in Seller Central and total the units and their value. That is inventory inside Amazon's warehouses that is unsellable and accruing storage.
  • Sort by reason: each of the five reasons above needs a different fix.
  • Price it: match each stranded ASIN against your storage-fee report to see what leaving it unresolved costs per month.
  • Check the age: compare each stranded unit against Amazon's current aged-inventory threshold. Units close to the line come first.
  • Assign it: give each ASIN an owner and a date, and write down whether the plan is fix, remove or dispose.
  • Repeat: monthly, and weekly going into the fourth quarter.

For example, if a stranded ASIN holds stock whose storage cost is a few dollars a month, it is easy to defer. The same ASIN deferred across a peak-season rate change and an aged-surcharge date is not. The calendar, not the unit count, is what makes the cost grow.

What to do once inventory has stranded

Diagnose the specific stranding reason first. Seller Central's report names it, and the fix is entirely different depending on which one applies. A restricted ASIN needs documentation or category approval, not a listing edit. A listing error needs the specific catalog conflict resolved, often a category or attribute correction. A closed or under-review listing needs the underlying compliance issue addressed before Amazon will reactivate it. In every case storage keeps accruing while the fix is in progress, so speed is valuable. Delay does not make the fix harder, but it is its own additional cost.

Once the issue is fixed, resist the urge to make every other pending change to the listing at the same time. A listing that just cleared a stranding review is being watched more closely than usual, and a burst of unrelated edits right after reinstatement can invite a second flag before the first has fully cleared. Fix the strand first, make everything else after, and leave a few days of observation in between.

If a listing has been stranded long enough that removal or disposal makes more financial sense than paying storage on unsellable stock, that is a legitimate call. It should be a deliberate one, made after checking whether the real fix is close, not the default outcome of the problem never being addressed. Fees that are not storage: check the box, too

While you are auditing stranded stock, check the other fee that depends on a physical fact about the unit. Amazon bills fulfillment by dimensional weight when it exceeds actual weight, so the box can set the fee rather than the product. In one real case from our own accounts, a unit weighing 1.119 lb in a 6.77 x 6.38 x 6.06 in box is billed at 2.00 lb. That is a reported-internally example from our accounts, not a typical result, but it shows why measurements held in the catalog deserve the same standing check as the stranded report.

Which one you should actually pick

Stranded inventory is rarely a single event worth panicking over. It is a slow, compounding cost that grows the longer the listing issue goes unaddressed, and faster in the fourth quarter. A monthly stranded-inventory check, run on the same cadence as a fee audit, keeps it small. Sellers with large catalogs benefit most from outside monitoring; small catalogs can often manage it in-house.

Common questions

Do I still pay Amazon storage fees on stranded inventory?

Yes. Storage fees are charged on the physical space inventory occupies in an Amazon warehouse, regardless of whether the listing above it is sellable. Stranded inventory accrues storage exactly as if it were active.

Can stranded inventory trigger the aged-inventory surcharge too?

Indirectly. The surcharge is based on how long inventory has sat unsold past a threshold, and stranded inventory cannot sell by definition. The longer it stays stranded, the more likely it crosses that threshold and picks up the surcharge on top of ordinary storage.

Why does stranded inventory cost more in the fourth quarter?

Amazon raises storage rates for the holiday season. A unit that is still stranded when the peak rate starts pays the higher rate while earning nothing, so the same stranding costs more the later in the year it is resolved. Check Amazon's current fee schedule for live rates.

How do I find out how much stranded inventory I have?

Seller Central's Stranded Inventory report shows affected ASINs and the specific stranding reason for each. Total the units and their value, then cross-reference against your storage-fee report to see the ongoing cost of leaving it unresolved.

Should I remove stranded inventory instead of fixing the listing?

Sometimes, if the fix is genuinely far off and storage costs are outpacing the inventory's value. It should be a deliberate decision made after checking how close the real fix is, not a default that happens because nobody addressed the stranding reason.
