What counts as a counterfeit on Amazon

A counterfeit is a product that copies a genuine brand's trademark, packaging or logo without the brand owner's permission, and passes itself off as the real thing. Amazon's anti-counterfeiting policy prohibits the sale of these items, and it applies to every seller on the marketplace, whether they ship from their own warehouse or use Fulfilled by Amazon.

Three things are often mixed up with counterfeits, and the difference matters when you decide what to do:

  • Counterfeit: a fake that uses someone else's brand. This is the policy violation.
  • Gray market: a genuine product sold outside the brand's intended channel, such as a different country or an unauthorised reseller. The product is real, so it usually needs a different kind of complaint.
  • Inauthentic or not as described: a listing or item that does not match what was promised. Some are counterfeits and some are simple quality or listing errors.

Getting the label right shapes the report. A report that says exactly what is fake, and why, is easier for Amazon to act on than one that says only that the item looks wrong.

How fakes end up in the listing

Most pages that rank for this topic describe the problem from the buyer's side. It helps to understand the mechanics, because they explain why a fake can show up next to a genuine listing for the same product.

Amazon's catalogue is built around one detail page per product. Several sellers can offer the same ASIN, and they compete for the Buy Box on that one page. That is good for choice and price. It also means a seller who ships a fake can sit on a genuine product's page and take the sale, and the buyer sees the brand's name, images and reviews.

Fulfilled by Amazon adds a second route. When inventory for the same product is held in more than one place, units from different sellers can be stored together. That is a reason for brand owners to watch their own pages and to look at what customers receive, not only at what the listing says.

For the brand, the cost is rarely just one lost sale. The customer who receives a fake blames the brand, the complaint lands on the genuine page, and the review or return is attached to the product the brand has built.

How buyers can spot a fake before and after ordering

None of these checks is proof on its own. Together they cut the risk a lot.

  • Check who is selling. On the product page, look at who ships and who sells. If the brand sells its own product on Amazon, a seller you have never heard of offering the same item is worth a second look.
  • Compare against the brand's own site. Look at the official images, pack sizes, and variants. If a listing offers a version the brand does not make, treat it with caution.
  • Be wary of prices far below the usual range. A large gap with no explanation is a common sign.
  • Read the recent reviews, not just the average. Look for repeated mentions of packaging, smell, texture, serial numbers or missing seals.
  • Inspect on arrival. Check the packaging, batch codes, print quality and any security seal against the brand's guidance. If the product is something you ingest or apply to skin, stop using it if anything seems off.

If you think you received a fake, keep the packaging and the item, take photographs, and use the report option on your order or the product page. Amazon's customer service can also guide you on returns and refunds.

What brand owners can do: report, prove and prevent

Brand owners have more tools than buyers, and the first one is Brand Registry. Enrolling a registered trademark gives access to Amazon's reporting tools, which let the brand file infringement reports directly rather than relying on customer complaints. Amazon also runs programs such as Transparency, which uses unique codes on units so that genuine items can be verified, though availability and rules change, so check Amazon's own pages for current terms.

A practical routine looks like this:

  • Monitor your own listings. Check the offers on each ASIN regularly for sellers you do not recognise, and keep a log of what you saw and when.
  • Buy a test unit. If a suspect offer appears, ordering a unit gives you the actual item, the packaging and the order record.
  • Gather evidence. Photographs, the order number, the seller name and a short note on exactly what differs from the genuine product make a report specific.
  • File through Brand Registry. Name the ASIN, the seller and the trademark involved, and attach the evidence.
  • Follow up and record the outcome. Reports are sometimes rejected or need more detail. Keeping a record helps when the same seller returns.

Prevention matters as much as removal. A clear trademark registration, accurate detail pages, consistent packaging, and a list of your authorised resellers all make it easier to show that an offer is not yours.

Reviews and complaints: what can and cannot be reported

Fakes tend to bring bad reviews with them, and it is tempting to treat every negative review as removable. It is not. Amazon removes reviews that break its community guidelines, such as abuse, promotional spam, seller or shipping content on a product page, or disclosed personal data. A review that is simply negative about the product, including a review from a customer who received a counterfeit, stays up unless it breaks those rules.

The better long-term fix is to remove the cause. Once the fake offer is gone, new buyers receive genuine products, and the brand can answer the original complaint publicly and honestly. Anything that tries to manipulate reviews or rankings breaks Amazon's terms and puts the brand's own account at risk, so it is not a shortcut worth considering.

When it is a seller account problem, too

There is one more side of this topic that the buyer guides skip. Sellers can be accused of counterfeiting when they are not guilty, for example when they resell genuine stock and a brand or a customer complains. Amazon takes these complaints seriously, and an unresolved one can lead to listings being removed or an account being suspended.

The response is the same in both directions: evidence. A seller who can show invoices from the brand or an authorised distributor, with matching quantities and dates, has a far stronger case than one who simply says the stock is genuine. Brand owners who report offers should expect that a legitimate reseller may reply with exactly this proof, and be ready to check it honestly.

Common questions

How do I report counterfeit products on Amazon?

Buyers can use the report option on the product page or from their order. Brand owners enrolled in Brand Registry can file infringement reports directly, naming the ASIN, the seller and the trademark involved, with supporting evidence.

Does Amazon sell counterfeit goods?

Amazon prohibits counterfeits in its policy, but because third-party sellers share product pages, fakes can still appear. Reporting them is how they get reviewed and removed.

How can I tell if a product on Amazon is fake?

Check who is selling it, compare it against the brand's own images and variants, be careful with prices far below the normal range, and read recent reviews for repeated mentions of packaging or quality. Inspect the item on arrival.

What should I do if I already received a fake item?

Keep the item and packaging, take photographs, stop using it if it is something you ingest or apply to skin, and report it through your order. You can then request a return or refund.

Can a brand get negative reviews from a counterfeit removed?

Only if the review breaks Amazon's community guidelines. A review that is negative about the product, even one caused by a fake, is not removable. Removing the fake offer is the lasting fix.
