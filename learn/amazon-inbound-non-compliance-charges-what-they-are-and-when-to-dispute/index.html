Amazon Inbound Non-Compliance Charges: What They Are and When to Dispute

Updated November 3, 2026 · By Ken Freeman · 1813 words · Written against what currently ranked for “Amazon inbound non-compliance charges: what they are and when to dispute”
The short answer

An inbound non-compliance charge is a fee Amazon applies when a shipment doesn't match its shipping plan: wrong labeling, incorrect packaging, a case-pack mismatch, or an ASN that doesn't reflect what arrived. Dispute it only when your own packing records disagree with Amazon's receiving record; if your records agree with Amazon, it is owed.

What triggers a non-compliance charge

These charges cluster around a handful of causes: units missing the FNSKU or barcode label the shipping plan specified, a shipment packed in a case configuration that doesn't match what was declared, an advance shipping notice (ASN) whose quantities don't reconcile with what the receiving center actually counted, or pallet and box labeling that doesn't meet Amazon's inbound requirements. Each is charged because it creates extra manual work at the receiving fulfillment center. Someone has to physically sort out a mismatch that automated receiving can't process on its own, and the fee is meant to offset that labor.

The common thread is a data mismatch between what your shipping plan declared and what physically showed up. Automated receiving scans and matches against an expected record; anything that doesn't match routes to manual handling, and manual handling is what the fee is priced against. Understanding that mechanism is what separates a preventable charge from an inevitable one: most of this category is about keeping your declared shipping plan and your physical shipment in sync, not about dodging an arbitrary penalty.

Amazon sets the exact fee amounts and the list of chargeable defects, and both are revised from time to time. Check the current inbound requirements and fee schedule in Seller Central before you rely on any figure you read elsewhere, including here.

Why this category runs at real volume across a managed book

Inbound non-compliance records build up on any account that ships to Amazon regularly. Your own inbound performance history in Seller Central shows how many you have. It is a routine byproduct of running inbound shipments at volume, across multiple 3PLs, packers and shipment cycles, where a labeling template gets slightly out of date or a case-pack count gets keyed wrong once in a while.

Most of these charges are individually small, which is exactly what makes them easy to accumulate unnoticed. That is why the category needs a standing check rather than a reactive one. For scale, here is where the money has actually come back on the accounts we manage: across our measured portfolio, lost inbound inventory accounted for $215,676 of Amazon reimbursements across 1,271 items between 10 Feb and 21 Aug 2026. Inbound is a place where Amazon's records and a seller's records genuinely diverge, and it pays to compare them.

Which of these are genuinely owed, and which are worth disputing

A charge tied to a real packing or labeling error on your end, such as units that genuinely shipped without the correct FNSKU label, is legitimately owed. Disputing it wastes a case without changing the outcome. The disputable category is different: an ASN quantity mismatch where your own shipment record and the receiving center's count disagree, a charge applied against the wrong shipment ID entirely, or a labeling charge on units your own photos and packing list show were correctly labeled before they left your facility.

The distinguishing question is whether you hold your own contemporaneous record (a packing list, label photos, a carrier manifest) that documents what actually left your dock. That is the evidence a dispute needs.

A worked example of a disputable case

For example, a shipment of 200 units ships with an ASN declaring 200 units across 4 cases of 50. The receiving center's count comes back at 196, and a non-compliance charge posts against the 4-unit shortfall as a case-pack discrepancy. If your own warehouse packing log and carrier bill of lading both confirm 200 units left on the declared case configuration, that's a genuine dispute: the discrepancy happened somewhere in transit or at receiving, not at your end, and the documentation to prove it already exists in your own records. Filed with the packing log and carrier documentation attached, this is the kind of case that can be corrected. Filed with nothing but "we packed it correctly," it is the kind that gets denied for lack of evidence.

Compare that with a non-disputable version of the same charge: the same shipment, but your packing log shows only 196 units were loaded because a case was miscounted before it left your dock. Here Amazon's receiving count is correct and the ASN was wrong. The charge is legitimately owed, and the real fix is catching the miscount at your own end next time. The evidence you already hold tells you which version you're in before you file anything.

A dispute checklist: what to gather before you file

Work through this list in order. If you can't tick the first three, you probably don't have a dispute yet.

  • The charge itself: the shipment ID, the date it posted, the reason Amazon gives and the amount.
  • Your shipping plan and ASN: the quantities and case configuration you declared, exactly as submitted.
  • Your packing log: the count and case configuration at the moment the shipment left your dock, signed or timestamped.
  • Label photos: dated photos showing FNSKU placement on units and labeling on boxes or pallets.
  • Carrier paperwork: the bill of lading or manifest with the carton or pallet count.
  • Amazon's receiving record: the received quantity for that shipment, so you can state the exact gap.
  • One clear sentence: what Amazon's record says, what yours says, and the difference.

File one case per shipment with the evidence attached, and keep the answer short and factual. A tidy case with documents is easier for a reviewer to act on than a long narrative. No dispute outcome is certain, so file only where the records genuinely support you.

Why this category is easy to either over-dispute or ignore entirely

Sellers tend toward one of two failure modes here, and both are costly. Some dispute every non-compliance charge on principle, without checking whether their own records support the dispute. That wastes case history on charges that were legitimately owed and erodes credibility with Seller Support over time. Others assume every one of these charges is simply the cost of doing business and never check at all, which leaves genuine data-mismatch errors unclaimed. The evidence-first approach avoids both: check your own record before deciding, rather than defaulting to fighting everything or accepting everything.

What to do to reduce these before they happen

Most non-compliance charges are cheaper to prevent than to dispute after the fact. A pre-ship checklist that confirms label placement against the current Amazon spec, a case-pack count that's double-verified against the ASN before the shipment leaves the dock, and dated photos of pallet labeling kept as a standing practice all reduce how many charges show up, and they're exactly the documentation a dispute needs when one posts anyway.

If you work through a third-party prep center or a 3PL before goods reach Amazon, the checklist needs to live there too. A labeling error introduced at a prep center shows up on your account exactly the same as one made in-house, and Amazon's charge doesn't distinguish who packed the box. Build the same pre-ship verification, and the same photo habit, into any outsourced prep step.

Which one you should actually pick

Not every inbound non-compliance charge is worth fighting. Some are legitimately owed, and disputing those wastes a case. Dispute when your own shipping records disagree with Amazon's receiving count, and accept the charge when they agree. Keeping that documentation as a routine habit, not just after a charge posts, is what makes a dispute winnable.

Common questions

What causes an Amazon inbound non-compliance fee?

Most commonly a labeling mismatch, an incorrect case-pack configuration, or an ASN quantity that doesn't reconcile with what the receiving fulfillment center actually counted for that shipment.

Can I dispute an Amazon inbound non-compliance charge?

Yes, if you have your own contemporaneous documentation (a packing list, label photos, or a carrier manifest) showing the shipment left correctly configured. A charge tied to a genuine packing error on your end is legitimately owed and not worth disputing.

How do I prevent inbound non-compliance charges?

A pre-ship checklist verifying label placement against the current Amazon spec, double-checking case-pack counts against the ASN before shipping, and keeping dated photos of pallet labeling as a standing practice all reduce how often these charges post in the first place.

Are inbound non-compliance charges the same as fulfillment fees?

No. They're a separate charge tied to a shipment not matching its declared plan, distinct from the per-unit fulfillment fee that's set by size tier and weight.

What evidence do I need to win an inbound non-compliance dispute?

Documentation from before the shipment left your facility, such as a packing list, label photos, or a carrier bill of lading, that shows what actually shipped and can be compared against Amazon's receiving record.
