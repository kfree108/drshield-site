The short answer

A size-tier misclassification means Amazon bills a product at a higher fulfillment tier than its real packaged size, and the extra fee repeats on every unit sold. Catch it by measuring the sealed, shipped package against the catalog record after any packaging change, and on a quarterly cycle.

How a size tier assignment actually gets set

Amazon assigns a size tier from the dimensions and weight recorded for the product at listing setup, then re-checks it periodically against actual shipped packages and against the greater of actual or dimensional weight. That re-check isn't continuous or immediate. It happens on Amazon's own schedule, not the moment your packaging changes, which is exactly the gap that lets a misclassification persist for months. Amazon's current fee tables are on its seller pricing page; check the tier boundaries there against your own measurements rather than relying on a number you remember.

Why a tier change is worse than a fee change

A one-time reimbursement claim recovers a specific, bounded loss. A size-tier misclassification is different in kind: it's a rate error that applies to every future unit sold until someone notices, so the total cost keeps growing the longer it goes unchecked. A product selling 500 units a month, overcharged by even a modest per-unit amount because it's classified one tier too high, doesn't lose that amount once. It loses it 500 times a month, every month.

That's why tier misclassification deserves a different kind of check than a reimbursement audit. Reimbursements look backward at what already happened; a tier check looks at what's about to keep happening.

How a product drifts between tiers without anyone deciding it should

Four patterns account for most of the drift we see:

  • A packaging change. A new supplier's box, an added insert or extra cushioning pushes dimensional weight over a tier boundary.
  • A stale catalog record. The dimensions on file were accurate at launch and were never updated after a real packaging change.
  • A bundle or multi-pack. It's created from an existing single-unit listing, and the combined package's dimensions are estimated rather than measured, so it lands in the wrong tier from day one.
  • Dual sourcing. On private-label catalogs that use a backup supplier for the same SKU, two factories rarely produce identical packaging even against the same spec sheet. A tier set against one supplier's dimensions can be wrong the moment the second supplier's units ship, and nobody logs it as a packaging change because, on paper, it's the same product.

The box, not the product, sets the fee. In our own accounts we've seen a unit weighing 1.119 lb in a 6.77 x 6.38 x 6.06 in box billed at 2.00 lb. The product didn't change; the carton decided the charge.

A worked example of what drift costs over a year

For example, take a product selling 400 units a month that quietly crosses a size-tier boundary in month one and isn't caught until month twelve. If the tier step costs $1.50 more per unit (an illustrative gap, not a quoted Amazon rate), the arithmetic is:

  • 400 units x $1.50 = $600 a month in avoidable fee
  • $600 x 11 months unnoticed = $6,600

Nothing about that $600 shows up as an alarming number on a settlement report. It reads as a normal fluctuation in fulfillment costs unless someone is specifically watching the tier assignment against the shipped package, not just the total fee line. To run it on your own SKU: monthly units x per-unit tier gap x months since the packaging last changed.

How to actually catch this: a checklist

Pull your current fee report and, for your highest-volume SKUs first, work through this list:

  • Measure the actual shipped, sealed package, not the spec sheet and not the dimensions from the original listing setup.
  • Weigh it, and calculate dimensional weight from the measured box so you can see which weight the fee follows.
  • Compare the result with the size tier and dimensions Amazon has on file for that ASIN.
  • Photograph the package with a tape measure and a scale, and date the photos.
  • Record the result per SKU so next quarter's check has a baseline.

Run it on a standing quarterly cadence, and also on trigger events: a supplier change, a new packaging vendor, a redesigned bundle, a new variant built from an existing listing, or even a small cosmetic update to the box. Each is a moment the physical product changed while the catalog record almost certainly didn't. Tying the check to the event, not just the calendar, closes months of drift.

What to do when a misclassification is confirmed

If your measurement shows the product should sit in a lower tier than Amazon has on file, that's a fee dispute with strong evidence: dated, photographed measurements against the current record. Before filing, run the same check across a wider window than today. If the packaging genuinely changed recently, the current higher tier may be correct now even if it wasn't before, and a dispute against an accurate current measurement won't succeed. If the record is simply stale and the product was always in the lower tier, file the dispute with dated evidence and separately update the catalog record so the same drift doesn't recur on that SKU.

A size-tier error moves only the fulfillment fee. It doesn't touch the referral fee, which is set by category and price rather than dimensions. So if margin has eroded and the referral percentage hasn't changed, the fulfillment side, and size tier specifically, is the first place to look.

Spotting drift in your numbers before you measure anything

You can often flag a suspect SKU from data you already have, before anyone picks up a tape measure. Three signals are worth a look:

  • Fulfillment cost per unit rising while sales mix is flat. If the same SKU costs more to fulfill this month than last and nothing about price or destination changed, the tier or weight on file is the first suspect.
  • Two near-identical SKUs billed differently. A single and its multi-pack, or two colour variants from the same factory, should sit in predictable tiers. An odd one out deserves a measurement.
  • Billed weight that doesn't match the box. Because the fee follows the greater of actual and dimensional weight, a billed weight well above both numbers you calculate means the record on file is wrong. In one of our own accounts, a unit weighing 1.119 lb was billed at 2.00 lb for exactly this reason.

None of these proves an error. They tell you which SKUs to measure first, which is the cheapest way to spend an afternoon on this.

Which one you should actually pick

A one-time fee dispute fixes a single overcharge. A size-tier check catches the error that keeps recurring until someone looks, so build it into a quarterly routine on your highest-volume SKUs, where the same small per-unit gap compounds most. Sellers with few, stable products can run the checklist themselves; larger catalogs with frequent packaging changes may prefer an ongoing audit.

Common questions

What is an Amazon size tier misclassification?

A mismatch between the size tier Amazon's system has on file for a product and its true dimensions and weight. It usually happens after a packaging change or a bundle creation that wasn't followed by a fresh measurement check.

How much does a size-tier misclassification actually cost?

It varies by tier boundary and sales volume, but because the error repeats on every unit sold, even a modest per-unit gap adds up. For example, a $1.50 gap on 400 units a month is $600 a month, or $6,600 over 11 months.

How often should I check my products' size tiers?

Any time packaging changes on the supplier side or a new bundle or variant is created, plus a standing quarterly check regardless. Catalog records don't update themselves when a physical product does.

Why was my product's size tier changed by Amazon?

Amazon re-checks tiers against shipped packages and the greater of actual or dimensional weight, on its own schedule. If a packaging change, a second supplier or a new bundle altered the real carton, the re-check may move the product to a new tier. Measure the sealed package and compare it with the record before assuming either side is wrong.

Can I fix a size-tier misclassification myself?

Yes. Measure the current shipped, sealed package and compare it against the tier Amazon has on file. If they don't match, file a fee dispute with dated, photographed measurements, and separately update the catalog record so the drift doesn't recur.
