Amazon Account Health Rating: Reading the Score and Acting On It

Updated 2026-08-22 · 1524 words · Written against what currently ranked for “Amazon Account Health: reading the rating and acting on it”
The short answer

Amazon's Account Health Rating (AHR) is a 0–1,000 score: 200 or above is healthy, 100–199 is at risk of deactivation, and 99 or below means the account is eligible for deactivation or already deactivated. New sellers start at 200. The score itself doesn't say what to fix — the flags underneath it do.

What the number actually measures

AHR isn't a single metric like order defect rate or late shipment rate — it's a composite score Amazon computes from your account's overall policy compliance and performance history, weighted toward more recent activity. It's meant to be a fast, at-a-glance read of standing, the way a credit score summarizes a lot of underlying financial behavior into one number. That summary quality is also its limitation: two accounts sitting at the same AHR can have completely different underlying problems, one with a single serious violation and another with several smaller performance issues accumulating.

Because of that, the number is a starting point, not a diagnosis. It tells you roughly how urgent your situation is; the Account Health dashboard underneath it — broken out by policy compliance, product policy compliance, and customer service performance — is where the actual, specific issue lives.

It's also worth knowing the score is account-wide, not per-listing. A single problematic ASIN in an otherwise clean catalog can drag the whole account's rating down, which is part of why an isolated issue is worth fixing promptly even when the rest of the business looks fine — the number doesn't distinguish between one bad listing and a systemic problem on its own.

The three bands, and what each genuinely calls for

At 200 and above — the healthy, green band — the useful discipline is monitoring, not panic. Check the dashboard regularly enough to catch a new flag early, since the score reflects a trailing window and a single new issue can start pulling it down before it's obviously visible day to day.

At 100 to 199 — the at-risk, yellow band — the account isn't deactivated, but it's genuinely exposed, and the right response is triage: identify every open flag on the dashboard, prioritize the ones tied to the most serious violation categories, and resolve them methodically rather than picking whichever feels easiest first. At 99 and below — the red band — the account is eligible for deactivation if it isn't already deactivated, and every open issue needs a documented, evidenced response, submitted through the correct channel for its type, as close to immediately as the evidence allows.

The mistake sellers make with a healthy score

A score sitting comfortably in the green band produces a natural complacency — the dashboard isn't checked for weeks, because there's no obvious reason to. The problem is that AHR reflects a trailing window, which means a real, current problem can exist for days before it's meaningfully visible in the aggregate number. A seller who only checks the score, rather than the underlying flags, discovers a serious issue later than they would have by watching the dashboard directly.

The healthier habit is treating AHR as a lagging confirmation, and the individual policy and performance sections as the leading indicator worth actually watching on a schedule — weekly is a reasonable cadence for most accounts, more often for one carrying several ASINs in higher-complaint categories.

The other version of this mistake is watching only the topline number and never opening the sub-categories at all. Policy compliance, product policy compliance, and customer service performance can each be trending in different directions under a stable-looking aggregate score, and the one quietly worsening is the one worth catching early, before it drags the overall number down with it.

When a single flag is worth acting on immediately, versus monitoring

Not every flag on the dashboard needs an emergency response. A single, minor performance metric drifting slightly is often worth watching for a cycle before escalating internally, since some fluctuation is normal and chasing every small movement wastes attention better spent elsewhere. A flag tied to authenticity, safety, or a serious policy violation is different — those are the categories most likely to move an account toward the at-risk or deactivation-eligible bands quickly, and they warrant the same case-management discipline as any other serious Amazon case: one case ID, evidence gathered specifically for that violation type, and a documented response rather than a delayed one.

The practical filter is severity, not recency: a flag that could plausibly lead to deactivation on its own gets immediate, evidenced attention regardless of how the rest of the dashboard looks. A flag that's clearly minor and isolated can reasonably wait for the next scheduled review, as long as that review actually happens on schedule rather than being deferred indefinitely.

What to do when the score drops and the cause isn't obvious

Sometimes the dashboard shows a drop without an obviously new, individual flag explaining it — several smaller issues compounding rather than one clear cause. In that situation, work backward through every open item on the dashboard, oldest first, rather than assuming the most recent one is the culprit; a score decline is often the cumulative effect of several unresolved smaller issues, not one dramatic new one. Resolving the oldest open items first tends to move the trailing score more than chasing whichever flag is newest.

It's worth resisting the urge to fix the most visible or most recent item first purely because it's the one currently drawing attention. A methodical pass through every open item, prioritized by how long it's been sitting and how serious its category is, is slower to start but more likely to actually move the score than a series of one-off fixes chosen by whichever flag feels most urgent that day.

Which one you should actually pick

A healthy AHR suits a seller running a regular dashboard check and resolving small issues as they appear, rather than waiting for the topline number to move before paying attention. An at-risk or deactivation-eligible score calls for methodical, evidenced casework on every open flag, oldest and most serious first — and neither the score nor any single fix guarantees Amazon's next decision, since Amazon evaluates each underlying flag on its own facts and its own timeline.

Common questions

What is a good Amazon Account Health Rating?

200 or above is the healthy band, meaning the account isn't at risk of deactivation. New seller accounts start at 200. Below that, 100 to 199 is at risk, and 99 or below is eligible for deactivation or already deactivated.

Does a high Account Health Rating mean there are no problems?

Not necessarily. AHR reflects a trailing window of activity, so a recent issue can exist for days before it meaningfully moves the score. Checking the individual policy and performance flags directly is more reliable than watching the number alone.

How is Amazon's Account Health Rating calculated?

It's a composite 0–1,000 score built from an account's overall policy compliance and performance history, weighted toward more recent activity, rather than a single metric like late shipment rate on its own.

What should I do if my Account Health Rating drops into the at-risk band?

Triage every open flag on the Account Health dashboard, starting with the most serious violation categories, and resolve them with evidence through the correct channel rather than addressing whichever feels easiest first.

What score does a brand-new Amazon seller account start with?

200 — inside the healthy band. From there, the score moves based on the account's actual policy compliance and performance over the following months, not a fixed starting handicap.

How often should I check my Account Health dashboard?

Weekly is a reasonable baseline for most accounts, and more often for one running several ASINs in categories that see frequent complaints or policy scrutiny. The score itself lags real events, so checking the underlying flags directly matters more than watching the number.

Can one bad listing lower my whole account's health rating?

Yes. AHR is account-wide, not per-listing, so a single problematic ASIN can pull down the score for an otherwise clean catalog — which is why an isolated issue is worth resolving promptly rather than treated as low priority.
