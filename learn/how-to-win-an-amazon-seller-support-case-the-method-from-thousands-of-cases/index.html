Why most sellers lose cases they should have won

The typical failure mode isn't a bad Plan of Action or a weak argument. It's process: a seller opens a case, gets a templated denial, panics, opens a second case for the same issue, then a third, and by the time anyone reads the file closely there are three case IDs, three different reps' notes, and no single thread anyone can follow. Amazon's own support system is not built to reward persistence measured in case count — it's built to reward a clean, traceable record that a reviewer can follow start to finish without reconstructing what happened from three separate threads.

The method below isn't a theory. It's what's left after running enough of these cases to notice which habits correlate with a case actually moving, and which just generate more case numbers and more frustration. None of it is exotic. Most of it is the kind of administrative discipline that's easy to skip when you're annoyed at a templated denial and want to act immediately — which is exactly when skipping it costs the most.

Discipline one: one case ID, always

Every new case starts a fresh queue position and hands the file to a rep who has never seen it. If a case is open and unresolved, the instinct to open a second one for the same issue — because the first feels stuck, or because a different support channel seems like it might move faster — is almost always the wrong move. It splits your evidence across two threads, and whichever rep looks at either one sees a partial picture: half the documents, half the call history, and no way to know the other case exists unless you tell them, which most sellers forget to do under pressure.

The discipline is simple to state and hard to hold to under frustration: one issue, one case ID, and every new fact, document, or call note gets added to that same case, even when the wait is genuinely painful. If you're tempted to open a second case, that's usually the signal to escalate the first one instead — through internal review, or a specialist channel, not a duplicate.

Discipline two: call every three days, and make the rep document it

A case sitting in queue with no seller activity tends to sit longer than one that's being actively followed. Calling in for a status check roughly every three days — not daily, which reads as noise and can actually slow a rep down, and not only when a deadline is near — keeps the case visible and gives you a second data point beyond whatever's written in the file.

The part most sellers skip: before ending the call, ask the rep directly to read back what they've just logged in the case notes. A rep who has actually typed something meaningful in gives you a specific, checkable summary of what happens next. A rep who hasn't will stall, give a vague non-answer, or repeat a script — and that gap is itself useful information about whether the call actually moved anything or just consumed twenty minutes. Documenting the date, the rep's stated next step, and their read-back is the record you'll need if the case needs escalating later.

Discipline three: dig for the wrong-ASIN or wrong-order attachment

A meaningful share of stuck cases aren't stuck because the underlying argument is weak — they're stuck because somewhere upstream, an agent attached the wrong ASIN, the wrong order ID, or the wrong document to the case file, and every reviewer after that point is evaluating a file that doesn't match what you actually submitted. This is genuinely common, and it's rarely obvious from the outside, because the denial language usually still sounds like it's addressing your issue in general terms.

It's worth checking directly on every call: ask the rep to confirm, out loud, which ASIN and which order or shipment number the case is currently attached to, and compare it against what you actually submitted. Finding a mismatched attachment — and getting it corrected on the record, with a note in the case file confirming the correction — is one of the more common reasons a case that should have been simple keeps coming back denied for reasons that don't line up with your evidence at all. It's a five-minute check that a surprising number of open cases never get.

Discipline four: internal review over re-appeal

When a denial looks wrong on its face — the reviewer cited a policy that doesn't apply to your situation, or plainly didn't read a document that was clearly attached — the reflex is to hit the appeal or re-submit button again immediately. That's usually the wrong move, and it's the subject of a separate piece on why re-appealing an Amazon case restarts the clock. The short version here: a fresh submission frequently resets the review queue and routes to a new reviewer with no visibility into the history, rather than routing to someone positioned to look again at what's already on file.

Requesting internal review, by contrast, keeps the existing case record intact — every document, every call note, every prior reviewer's comment — and asks a different set of eyes to look at that same file with fresh judgment. It's slower to type, and it's the better first move almost every time a denial looks like an error rather than a genuine no.

A case log you can copy today

The disciplines only work if the record exists. Keep one row per case, in a spreadsheet or a notes file, and fill it in during the call rather than afterwards. For each case, write down:

  • Case ID and the single issue it covers (one issue, one ID).
  • ASIN and order or shipment ID you submitted, and the ones the rep says are attached.
  • Date and time of each contact, with the rep's first name or ID if given.
  • Next step the rep stated, in their words, from the read-back.
  • Documents sent, with file names and the date each went in.
  • Next call date, three days out.
  • Internal review requested? Yes or no, and the date.

When a case needs escalating, this log is the whole argument. It shows a reviewer that the case was followed, the evidence was complete and the mismatch, if there is one, was raised on a named date. It also shows you quickly when a case has had no real movement in three calls, which is the moment to ask for internal review instead of waiting another week.

Which one you should actually pick

The method suits any seller willing to run it consistently, on any case type: one case ID, a call cadence, documentation at every step, internal review before a fresh appeal. It suits sellers less well as a full-time discipline once the volume of open cases across listings, reimbursements, and account health has outgrown what one person can reasonably track by hand alongside everything else running a catalog requires — that's the point where managed casework earns its cost, and it's a genuinely poor fit for a single, straightforward case you're already handling closely and competently yourself with the five disciplines above.

Common questions

What's the single biggest mistake sellers make with Amazon cases?

Opening a second case for the same issue instead of building on the first one. It splits the evidence and the history across two case IDs, and no single reviewer ever sees the whole picture unless you point them to both — which most sellers, understandably, forget to do while frustrated.

How often should I call Amazon about an open case?

Roughly every three days is the useful cadence — enough to keep the case active and get a status read-back from the rep, without calling so often that it reads as noise rather than a genuine follow-up.

Does calling Amazon actually help move a case along?

It can, mainly because it creates a second record beyond the written file and lets you confirm the case is attached to the correct ASIN and order — a mismatch there is a common, fixable cause of a stuck case that a purely written back-and-forth can miss entirely.

Should I re-appeal a denied Amazon case right away?

Usually not immediately. A fresh appeal can restart the review queue and hand the case to someone with no history on it. Requesting internal review of the existing case, with its record intact, is generally the better first move when a denial looks wrong rather than genuinely settled on the facts.

What should I write down on every call with Amazon seller support?

The case ID, the date and time, the rep's name or ID, the ASIN and order or shipment ID the case is attached to, and the next step the rep reads back from their notes. Add the date of your next call. That short log is what lets you escalate a stuck case with evidence instead of frustration.
