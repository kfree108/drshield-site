The three sections, and what belongs in each

Root cause answers what happened, when, and why — specific ASINs or order IDs, specific dates, and a mechanism (not a symptom). "Sales dropped" is a symptom; "a supplier packaging change on February 12 wasn't reflected in listing content" is a mechanism. Corrective action answers what you've already done about the specific instances flagged — written in completed tense, with a date, and ideally referencing an attached record. Preventive action answers what stops this from happening again — a named, ongoing process with an owner or a system, not a general intention. A plan missing any one of the three reads as incomplete to a reviewer even if the other two are strong, because the reviewer is checking for all three before deciding.

Order matters less than completeness — some sellers combine root cause and corrective action into one paragraph when they're tightly linked, and that's fine as long as all three elements are present somewhere in the submission and clearly identifiable, rather than blended into a single undifferentiated block of text a reviewer has to parse for the pieces themselves.

Submission mechanics that trip people up

The plan of action is submitted through the case associated with your specific notice — usually Account Health or the Case Log — not emailed, not attached as a separate support ticket, and not sent through a general inquiry. Most notices accept plain text in the case's response field or a PDF attachment for longer submissions with supporting documents. Keep supporting evidence — invoices, screenshots, correction logs — as clearly labeled separate attachments rather than embedded as unreadable low-resolution images inside the plan text itself; a reviewer working through a queue of cases needs to open evidence quickly, and a plan that requires zooming into an embedded screenshot to read a date slows that down.

Name attachments descriptively rather than with a device's default filename — "supplier-invoice-feb2026.pdf" tells a reviewer what they're opening before they open it; "IMG_4471.jpg" doesn't. It's a small detail, but a case file that's easy to navigate reads as more credible than one a reviewer has to guess their way through.

A format checklist to run before you submit

Before submitting, check: does the root cause name a specific date and mechanism, not just a summary of the notice? Does the corrective action describe something completed, with a date, not something planned? Does the preventive action name an actual process or control, not a sentiment like "we'll be more careful"? Is every claim backed by an attached, labeled piece of evidence a reviewer could check independently? Is the writing free of dispute language — arguing the violation shouldn't apply — since that belongs in a different channel, not the plan itself? A plan that passes all five checks on the first submission clears review meaningfully faster than one that has to be revised after a rejection reveals a gap.

Run the checklist against the notice too, not just your draft — confirm every specific ASIN, order ID, or instance the notice names is addressed somewhere in your plan. A checklist-clean plan that still misses one cited instance fails for a different, equally avoidable reason.

A worked example against the checklist

Take a suspension notice citing a customer complaint pattern across four order IDs. Root cause: "Reviewing the four flagged orders, three shipped with a substitute packaging insert added by our 3PL between February 8–14 that wasn't approved against our listing content; the fourth was a duplicate complaint on an already-corrected order." Corrective action: "We removed the substitute insert from all outbound shipments as of February 16 and issued refunds on the three affected orders on February 17." Preventive action: "As of February 18, our 3PL is required to flag any packaging substitution to us in writing before shipping, and we run a weekly spot-check of five random outbound units against current listing content." That's a complete, checkable submission against every item on the checklist above — four dates, one named mechanism, one completed correction, one ongoing control.

What to do when the format is right but the case still fails

A correctly formatted plan can still be rejected if the evidence doesn't actually support the specific instances named in the notice — a common failure is addressing the general pattern while missing one of the specific order IDs or ASINs cited. Re-read the original notice line by line and confirm every specific instance it names is addressed somewhere in your plan, not just the pattern behind them. If a rejection cites "insufficient information" without naming which section, the safest read is that the evidence wasn't specific or verifiable enough — strengthen documentation on all three sections rather than guessing which one was weak, and resubmit once, with the gaps actually closed rather than the wording rearranged.

One more format detail worth checking before resubmission: attachment limits. Some case types cap the number or size of files you can attach, and a plan that references "see attached" for evidence that didn't actually make it through the upload reads as unsupported even though you believed you'd included it. Confirm every referenced attachment shows in the submitted case before assuming the format was the problem.

Which one you should actually pick

The format is fixed and simple — root cause, corrective action, preventive action, each with dates and evidence — and almost every rejection traces back to one of the three sections being vague rather than the format being wrong. Common questions

What are the three required sections of an Amazon plan of action?

Root cause (what happened and why, with dates), corrective action (what's already been fixed, in completed tense), and preventive action (the ongoing system that prevents recurrence). All three are checked; missing one reads as incomplete.

Should I submit the plan of action as text or a PDF?

Either is generally accepted — plain text in the case response field for shorter submissions, a PDF when you have longer supporting detail. Keep supporting evidence as separate, clearly labeled attachments rather than embedded inside the plan text.

How specific do dates need to be?

Exact dates, not ranges like "last month." A reviewer checking your claim against your account's actual history needs a specific date to verify against — vague timing is one of the more common reasons a root cause reads as unconfirmed.

Can I use bullet points in a plan of action?

Yes, and for multi-instance cases it often helps clarity — as long as each bullet still contains a specific, checkable fact rather than a general statement. Structure helps a reviewer scan; vague bullets don't fix vague content.

Is there a character or word limit on the plan of action fields?

Limits vary by case type and aren't uniformly published, so keep each section tight and specific rather than assuming unlimited space — a plan that hits a hard limit mid-sentence and gets cut off is a preventable, avoidable failure.
