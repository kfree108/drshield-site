FBA Fee Audit: the Five Lines to Check on Every SKU

Updated November 4, 2026 · By Ken Freeman · 1586 words · Written against what currently ranked for “FBA fee audit: the five lines to check on every SKU”
The short answer

An FBA fee audit checks five lines on every SKU: the referral fee category, the fulfillment fee size tier, monthly storage, the aged-inventory surcharge, and any open reimbursement claims from the last 90 to 120 days. Checking only one line leaves the other four unverified.

The five lines at a glance

Use this as the checklist. Each line is a different question, and each needs different data from Seller Central.

  • 1. Referral fee: is the SKU in the right category, and is the right band applied to the right part of the price?
  • 2. Fulfillment fee size tier: does the tier Amazon has on file match the sealed package you actually ship?
  • 3. Monthly storage: does the billed cubic footage match the product, and was the seasonal rate applied correctly?
  • 4. Aged-inventory surcharge: does Amazon's aged report match what you know is sitting unsold?
  • 5. Open reimbursement gaps: are there refunds without returned units, or warehouse losses without a credit?

Run the five in one sitting per SKU, starting with your highest-volume products, because lines one and two repeat on every unit sold.

Why five lines and not one

Most sellers who audit fees at all check one line, usually the fulfillment fee, because it is the most visible. That leaves four lines unchecked. In our experience the money does not reliably sit in the fulfillment fee; it tends to sit in whichever line has not been looked at recently, on whichever SKU changed most recently.

A five-line check takes only a little longer than a one-line check, and the report joins overlap heavily, so the second to fifth lines are cheaper than the first. That is the argument for treating this as a checklist rather than one number to glance at.

Line one: the referral fee category and band

Referral fees are a percentage of the sale price that varies by category, and several categories charge in bands within the same sale rather than one flat rate. Amazon publishes the current rates on its seller pricing page; check the figures there rather than trusting a guide, including this one, because rates and category rules are revised.

The audit check is not "is my referral fee roughly right." It is two narrower questions. First, is the SKU assigned to the category you would expect? A listing that has drifted into a different classification can be over- or under-charged on every sale. Second, for any category with price bands, is the correct rate being applied to the correct portion of the price? Recalculate one order by hand per SKU and compare it with the settlement line.

Line two: the fulfillment fee size tier

This is the line most likely to have drifted silently, because nothing prompts a re-check when a supplier changes the packaging. Measure the actual shipped, sealed package, weigh it, and confirm the tier Amazon has on file matches.

Here is a real case from our own accounts. A unit weighing 1.119 lb, packed in a 6.77 x 6.38 x 6.06 in box, was billed at 2.00 lb. The box sets the fee, not the product. That is dimensional weight at work, and it is why the check is about the package, not the item. If a smaller carton is possible, the fee can change; if Amazon's recorded dimensions are wrong, that is a correction to request.

Line three: monthly storage fees

Storage is charged per cubic foot, monthly, and the rate is higher in the peak months at the end of the year than in the rest of the year. Check that the cubic footage being billed matches your actual product, and that a seasonal rate change has not been missed in a spreadsheet that only gets updated once a year.

Storage errors are usually smaller per unit than fulfillment errors, but they apply to every unit sitting in inventory, sold or not. Overstocked SKUs are therefore the most exposed here, so sort by units on hand and start from the top.

Line four: the aged-inventory surcharge

The exact day thresholds and dollar amounts for aged inventory change periodically, and a lot of published guidance is stale, so this page prints no figure. Read the current thresholds on Amazon's own pricing page before you assume last year's numbers still apply.

The audit check is structural. Pull your aged-inventory report, compare it with what you know has been sitting unsold, and flag any SKU approaching a threshold while there is still time to act on it. A slow seller that crosses a threshold unnoticed keeps accruing the surcharge. It is similar in shape to size-tier drift, but driven by the calendar rather than the packaging.

Line five: open reimbursement gaps from the last 90 to 120 days

A fee audit that finds an overcharge and a reimbursement audit that finds a lost unit are the same check run against different data: what should have happened versus what your settlement records show. Two patterns belong on this line. A refund issued in the last 60 to 120 days with no matching returned unit, and a warehouse loss reported in the last 60 days with no reimbursement credit.

This line is worth the time. Across the seller accounts we manage, we recovered $803,457 from Amazon between 10 Feb and 21 Aug 2026, net of $170,047 in reimbursement reversals (from $973,504 gross), across 42,096 line items. By value, the money came back like this:

  • Customer returns never returned: $508,269 (19,200 items)
  • Lost inbound: $215,676 (1,271 items)
  • Customer service issues: $102,858 (4,202 items)
  • Damaged in warehouse: $83,426 (5,594 items)
  • Lost in warehouse: $62,818 (5,233 items)

These are our own figures, from the Full Circle master database as of 21 Aug 2026, and they describe our managed portfolio, not a result any single seller should expect. The useful takeaway is where to look first: unreturned customer returns made up the largest share by value, and lost inbound shipments had the highest value per item.

Check which way a discrepancy runs

An audit that finds a discrepancy has to confirm its direction before anyone files anything. Sometimes a fee that looks like an overcharge turns out, at transaction level, to be an undercharge on some products. Disputing it would invite Amazon to correct the fee upward, not downward.

That is why the transaction-level check matters more than the settlement summary. Individual errors are rarely large enough to stand out in a summary, and the direction is only visible line by line. An audit that only ever reports discrepancies in the seller's favor is a one-sided audit, and it will eventually recommend a dispute that backfires. Where a discrepancy cuts the wrong way, the right advice is not to file.

Making the five-line check a habit

Run the five lines weekly on your highest-volume SKUs and monthly across the rest of the catalog. A fee audit done once a year finds last year's problem, not this quarter's.

Which one you should actually pick

Checking only the fulfillment fee misses most of what a real audit covers. A seller with a few SKUs can run all five lines by hand with the checklist above. A larger catalog benefits from a standing weekly and monthly audit, because the money sits in whichever line was not checked recently.

Common questions

What should an FBA fee audit check?

Five lines per SKU: the referral fee category and band, the fulfillment fee size tier against the actual shipped package, monthly storage accuracy, the aged-inventory surcharge status, and any open reimbursement gaps from the last 90 to 120 days.

How often should I run an FBA fee audit?

Weekly on your highest-volume SKUs and at least monthly across the rest of the catalog. Errors that compound, such as size-tier drift or an aged surcharge, get more expensive the longer they go unchecked.

Are Amazon referral fees a flat percentage?

Not always. The rate depends on the category, and some categories charge different rates on different portions of the sale price. Check the current rates on Amazon's seller pricing page, then recalculate one order per SKU to confirm.

Why is my fulfillment fee higher than the product weight suggests?

Amazon bills on the package, not just the item. In one case from our own accounts, a 1.119 lb unit in a 6.77 x 6.38 x 6.06 in box was billed at 2.00 lb. Measure the sealed package and compare it with the dimensions on file.

Is it always worth disputing a fee that looks too high?

No. Check the direction at transaction level first. A fee that looks like an overcharge can turn out to be an undercharge on some products, and a dispute could lead Amazon to raise it.
